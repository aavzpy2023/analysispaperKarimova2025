\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Predictive Modeling in Low-Data Bioactivity Regimes}

It is a well-established dogma in quantitative structure-activity relationships (QSAR) that parsimonious models generalize best \cite{winkler2026}. In data-limited scenarios, simpler interpretable approaches like multivariate linear regression have traditionally prevailed, often demonstrating prediction reliability equivalent to complex artificial neural networks \cite{hadas2023, xiewt2023}. These linear models maintain higher bias and lower variance, avoiding the extreme sensitivity to random fluctuations seen in complex algorithms, especially when supplemented by rigorous quantum chemical calculations to derive robust descriptors \cite{xiewt2023}.

Modeling small datasets is inherently susceptible to overfitting, a bottleneck severely exacerbated when the high dimensionality of molecular descriptors vastly exceeds the sample size \cite{hawkins2004, godarzi2013}. To combat this data sparsity, researchers frequently implement data augmentation (DAU) or synthetic sample generation to expand the training space \cite{gerz2026, tyler2025}. However, these techniques introduce non-negligible computational complexity and resource demands, often requiring high-performance hardware and iterative optimization cycles \cite{tyler2025, calle2025}. More critically, while DAU is designed to enhance model learning, its improper application often masks the true efficacy of the base algorithms by artificially elevating performance metrics through latent data dependencies and process-induced leakage \cite{gerz2026, john2025}.

Applying complex, non-linear architectures to these regimes introduces profound validation hurdles. When augmentation is applied globally before dataset partitioning ("augment-then-split"), it violates core independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumptions, allowing models to memorize augmentation artifacts rather than learn genuine physical rules \cite{gerz2026, kannoth2024}. This danger extends directly into model selection; tuning hyperparameters is exceptionally prone to overfitting when sample sizes are small and the parameter space is large \cite{cawley2010}. Consequently, evaluating machine learning architectures in isolation is meaningless without rigorously assessing the combined model selection procedure, making double (or nested) cross-validation mandatory to ensure true independence between model building and selection \cite{bauman2014, cawley2010}.

Traditional evaluation metrics and standard k-fold cross-validation often misleadingly overestimate explorative prediction capabilities \cite{kyrali2022, xiong2020}. Data augmentation strategies can exacerbate underlying data structures, yielding overly optimistic accuracy metrics that mislead stakeholders regarding the model’s true explorative power \cite{john2025}. Furthermore, algorithms like Random Forest fundamentally lack extrapolation capability \cite{xiong2020}, and generative deep learning faces severe limitations with sparse, discrete chemical spaces like SMILES strings \cite{yifan2023}. Therefore, to safeguard methodological integrity, any generative or augmentative process must be confined strictly to the training portion of the folds after a rigorous, independent partition has been established \cite{gerz2026, john2025}.

To safely harness the potential of overparameterized models \cite{winkler2026} without falling into the overfitting trap, rigorous validation protocols are mandatory. Techniques such as y-randomization (response shuffling) become essential to verify that the model captures true chemical relationships rather than random noise \cite{rucker2007}. Conversely, when descriptors are meticulously curated and validated, machine learning can effectively guide rational design, successfully discriminating potent bioactives—such as Amyloid-$\beta$ aggregation inhibitors—from decoys \cite{avantika2025}.

Methodologies like transfer learning bypass data scarcity by adapting base networks previously optimized on large source domains \cite{samuel2023}. By utilizing reduced learning rates during fine-tuning \cite{damilola2025}, and preferring efficient architectures like convolutional neural networks over heavier Transformer models \cite{riza2025}, researchers can robustly navigate class imbalance and model selection bias in low-resource settings.

\subsection{Domain-Engineered Features versus End-to-End Representation Learning}

Traditional feature-based machine learning relies heavily on domain-engineered descriptors and molecular fingerprints \cite{rajesh2026}. Methods like Morgan (ECFP4) encode structural traits, while topological, 3D, and physicochemical descriptors—such as radial distribution functions, Burden eigenvalues, and ab initio quantum chemical calculations—capture specific molecular interactions deterministically without requiring massive data \cite{avantika2025, beatrice2026, xiewt2023}. This approach embeds theoretical chemistry directly into the model, ensuring high interpretability and clarity \cite{xiewt2023}. However, accumulating these handcrafted features creates highly dimensional, redundant matrices \cite{godarzi2013, hadas2023}. In small datasets, this redundancy increases computational costs and severely worsens the feature selection bottleneck \cite{godarzi2013, hadas2023}.

Alternatively, end-to-end representation learning bypasses manual feature selection by autonomously mapping discrete molecular topologies into continuous latent spaces \cite{aga2025, abul2026}. Deep learning paradigms—including Autoencoders (VAEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)—extract task-specific traits directly from chemical structures to model complex, nonlinear interactions \cite{abul2026, rajesh2026, yifan2023}. Similarly, Chemical Language Processing (CLP) applies natural language techniques to string representations like SMILES \cite{riza2025}. However, learning representations in discrete chemical spaces is intrinsically fragile; altering even a single character in a SMILES string can irreparably invalidate the entire molecular structure \cite{yifan2023}.

Despite these advantages, the utility of deep learning complexity in low-data regimes remains highly contested. These algorithms are inherently data-hungry, making representation learning highly ineffective when ground-truth labels and dataset volumes are severely restricted \cite{sotirios2025}. To circumvent this limitation in the specific domain of \textit{Toxoplasma gondii} dihydrofolate reductase (TgDHFR) inhibition, recent studies have resorted to highly complex pipelines. For instance, \cite{karimova2025} employed Gaussian noise-based data augmentation and deep neural network (DNN) ensembles to stabilize training on a restricted dataset, elevating their baseline $R^2$ from 0.75 to 0.85. This raises a fundamental research question: is such statistical complexity and synthetic data generation strictly necessary to achieve competitive predictive reliability, or can a parsimonious classical machine learning ensemble achieve equivalent baseline performance ($R^2 \approx 0.75$) ``out-of-the-box'' using standard domain-engineered features? Because small datasets cannot naturally sustain deep architectures without risking catastrophic overfitting \cite{hadas2023, salman2023, samuel2023}, simpler classical ensembles frequently achieve equivalent or superior predictive reliability in data-scarce scenarios \cite{beatrice2026, rajesh2026, xiewt2023}.

Nevertheless, traditional skepticism toward nonlinear models in limited-data scenarios is changing \cite{Dalmau2025}. When properly tuned, regularized, and hybridized with structured linear priors or heuristics to stabilize training, nonlinear algorithms can achieve competitive results comparable to robust classical methods \cite{Dalmau2025, salman2023}. Furthermore, transfer learning serves as a vital catalyst. By fine-tuning pretrained networks like ChemBERTa, researchers can achieve accurate representation learning predictions even with only dozens of target data points \cite{latefa2025, samuel2023}.

\subsection{Methodological Rigor and the Threat of Data Leakage in Molecular Modeling}

Data leakage—the unintended introduction of test data information into the training process—is a statistically silent error that fundamentally undermines the validity of machine learning evaluations by inflating metrics without triggering code warnings \cite{brva2026, gerz2026}. In tabular molecular descriptor pipelines, this phenomenon typically manifests when global feature scaling (e.g., z-score standardization or MinMax scaling), descriptor imputation, or feature-filtering procedures (such as variance-threshold filtering or supervised feature selection) are executed prior to fold partitioning \cite{correa2025, lewis2023}. Applying scaling globally calculates dataset-wide statistics, such as the mean and standard deviation, which inadvertently leaks information regarding the test set distribution into the training process. Consequently, this violates the fundamental independence assumption between partitions and yields overoptimistic, biased performance estimates \cite{cawley2010}. Similarly, executing feature selection or variance-based filtering over the entire dataset introduces a selection bias, as the chosen predictors retain a partial "memory" of the hold-out test data \cite{varma2006}. 

To eliminate these vulnerabilities, preprocessing, scaling, and feature selection must be treated as integral components of the model-fitting workflow \cite{cawley2010}. All transformation parameters must be strictly computed within each inner training fold and subsequently applied to the validation and test folds without parameter re-estimation \cite{brva2026}. Rigorous partitioning protocols, such as strict three-way splits, must also be enforced to guarantee absolutely no overlap among training, validation, and test sets across subjects or discrete samples \cite{salman2023}.

Beyond preprocessing, data leakage frequently corrupts the model selection phase. Tuning hyperparameters globally across the entire dataset grants the algorithm illicit knowledge of the test distribution, yielding spuriously high performance \cite{kannoth2024}. Even in traditional internal validation, an overlap between the data used for hyperparameter tuning and the test set creates an optimistic bias that renders the evaluation uninformative regarding real-world generalizability \cite{john2025}. The validation dataset fundamentally loses its independence if its collective predictions continuously influence the search for an optimal model, triggering severe model selection bias. This lack of independence causes models—especially highly parameterized deep learning architectures deployed on small pretraining datasets \cite{sotirios2025}—to overfit by adapting to noise, producing deceptively overoptimistic internal metrics that severely underestimate true generalization errors \cite{bauman2014}.

To mitigate these structural biases, model selection must operate as an encapsulated component of the fitting process. Double or nested cross-validation is mandatory to generate strictly independent test objects in an outer loop that remain entirely isolated from model building and hyperparameter optimization \cite{bauman2014}. To further prevent fortuitous or biased splitting, researchers can employ deterministic methods like the Kennard-Stone algorithm to construct highly representative, unbiased external test sets \cite{xiewt2023}. When low-data regimes necessitate data augmentation, methodological purity remains paramount: classifier performance must be measured exclusively on real test data, keeping synthetic samples strictly isolated, while employing probabilistic Bayesian optimization rather than exhaustive grid searches to safely navigate the hyperparameter space \cite{tyler2025}. Preserving and transparently documenting this absolute methodological rigor is critical; insufficient reporting of data partitioning often confounds scientific literature, leading researchers to incorrectly attribute high performance to novel complex architectures when the success actually stems from silent data leakage \cite{rojas2026}.

\subsection{Objectives and Principal Contributions}

This study bridges the gap between stringent statistical validation and advanced representation learning to accelerate targeted drug discovery in low-data regimes. 

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Methodological Reproducibility and Leak-Free Validation:} Implementation of a fully transparent, 100\% reproducible Python pipeline built upon a rigorous 15-Fold Nested Cross-Validation protocol. This ensures absolute statistical independence between hyperparameter optimization and model evaluation, effectively eliminating model selection bias and data leakage.
	\item \textbf{Architectural Benchmarking in Data-Limited Regimes:} A comprehensive empirical comparison evaluating the predictive reliability of traditional domain-engineered classical ensembles against state-of-the-art Graph Neural Networks (specifically, the ChemProp Directed Message Passing Neural Network, D-MPNN) using a standardized reference dataset.
	\item \textbf{Empirical Validation of Parsimony over Complexity:} Explicit demonstration that simple classical machine learning ensembles achieve statistical parity ($R^2 \approx 0.75$) with baseline deep learning models reported in recent literature. This establishes that competitive predictive reliability can be attained "out-of-the-box," effectively eliminating the necessity for synthetic data augmentation and computationally expensive 3D geometric preprocessing in low-data regimes.
	\item \textbf{Translational Drug Repurposing Pipeline:} The seamless integration of machine learning predictions with structure-based Molecular Docking. This hybrid pipeline systematically screens, identifies, and prioritizes FDA-approved compounds, providing a validated computational pathway for rapid therapeutic repurposing.
\end{itemize}
