\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Role of Explicit Inductive Bias in Small-Scale Molecular Datasets}

Graph Neural Networks theoretically offer superior representational capacity by learning molecular features directly from graph topology, bypassing the need for manual feature engineering. However, this architectural flexibility demands extensive training data to converge on generalizable chemical representations. In the context of the TgDHFR dataset, which comprises only \NCompounds\ compounds, the GNN architecture heavily underperformed compared to classical machine learning approaches. 

This performance gap highlights the critical role of explicit inductive bias in low-data regimes. The fundamental lack of explicit chemical priors in deep representation learning forces these architectures to memorize the limited training set. To counteract this and force convergence, researchers are frequently compelled to inject artificial variance into the training pipeline; as observed in \cite{karimova2025}, deep neural networks on this dataset required extensive Gaussian noise-based data augmentation to achieve high predictive accuracy. 

Conversely, classical tree-based ensembles operating on pre-computed Morgan fingerprints bypass the need for end-to-end representation learning by utilizing fixed, domain-specific descriptors. These fingerprints encode established chemical heuristics---specifically, circular topological substructures---directly into the feature space. For small-scale datasets, this explicit pre-computation acts as a natural regularizer, rigidly constraining the hypothesis space and preventing the model from memorizing noise. Our results explicitly demonstrate that exploiting the inductive bias inherent to traditional structural fingerprints entirely eliminates the necessity for computationally burdensome data augmentation. When data volume is insufficient to natively train deep graph convolutions, deterministic feature engineering remains a vastly more reliable and elegant strategy for molecular property prediction.

\subsection{Dimensionality, Conformational Overhead, and Model Efficiency}

Beyond predictive accuracy, the computational efficiency of molecular representations dictates their practical utility in high-throughput virtual screening. Incorporating 3D geometric descriptors necessitates extensive conformational sampling and energy minimization (e.g., via ETKDGv3 embedding and MMFF94s optimization). This requirement introduces a severe computational bottleneck, termed conformational overhead, which scales exponentially as screening libraries expand into the millions of compounds.

Our empirical evaluation revealed that this added computational cost did not yield commensurate predictive gains. The highly engineered 2D/3D/FP representation achieved an $R^2$ of \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpRTwoMean, which was statistically indistinguishable from the purely structural Morgan fingerprint model ($R^2 = \MorganRTwoMean$, $p = \PairedTTestP$). This parity in performance indicates that the relevant pharmacophoric features governing TgDHFR inhibition are adequately captured solely by 2D topological connectivity. 

We explicitly frame this result as the "Advantage of Parsimony". By deliberately omitting complex 3D descriptors and synthetic data augmentation, our optimized classical pipeline circumvents massive computational expenditures during feature preprocessing and model training. Deploying computationally expensive 3D descriptors for this specific target introduces unnecessary dimensionality and processing latency without any tangible improvement in model fidelity. This strongly reinforces the superiority of lightweight, 2D fingerprint-based ensembles as the most efficient and scalable paradigm for virtual screening pipelines in low-data environments.

\subsection{Methodological Scope, Limitations, and Translational Potential}

The predictive reliability of any quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) model is inherently bounded by the chemical diversity of its training corpus. Given the dataset size of \NCompounds\ compounds, the applicability domain of our optimized ensemble is restricted to the structural scaffolds and physicochemical properties represented within the curated TgDHFR data from ChEMBL and BindingDB. Consequently, extrapolating predictions to highly divergent chemotypes or novel synthetic spaces carries an increased risk of reduced predictive confidence. 

Despite this limitation, the programmatic pipeline developed in this study offers substantial translational utility. By establishing a computationally lightweight, open-source framework, this approach democratizes high-throughput virtual screening. It functions as a highly efficient triage mechanism, rapidly filtering massive libraries of approved drugs to isolate a tractable subset of candidates. This targeted prioritization minimizes the computational expenditure required for subsequent physics-based simulations and reduces the attrition rate in downstream \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vivo} experimental validations.
