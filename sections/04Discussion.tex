\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Role of Explicit Inductive Bias in Small-Scale Molecular Datasets}

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) theoretically offer superior representational capacity by learning molecular features directly from graph topology, bypassing the need for manual feature engineering. However, this architectural flexibility demands extensive training data to converge on generalizable chemical representations. In the context of the TgDHFR dataset, which comprises only \NCompounds\ compounds, the GNN architecture underperformed compared to classical machine learning approaches. 

This performance gap highlights the critical role of explicit inductive bias in low-data regimes. Classical tree-based ensembles operating on pre-computed Morgan fingerprints bypass the need for end-to-end representation learning by utilizing fixed, domain-specific descriptors. These fingerprints encode established chemical heuristics---specifically, circular topological substructures---directly into the feature space. For small-scale datasets, this pre-computation acts as a natural regularizer, constraining the hypothesis space and preventing the model from memorizing noise. The results demonstrate that when data volume is insufficient to train deep graph convolutions effectively, leveraging the explicit inductive bias of traditional structural fingerprints remains a more robust strategy for molecular property prediction.

\subsection{Dimensionality, Conformational Overhead, and Model Efficiency}

Beyond predictive accuracy, the computational efficiency of molecular representations dictates their practical utility in high-throughput virtual screening. Incorporating 3D geometric descriptors necessitates extensive conformational sampling and energy minimization (e.g., via ETKDGv3 embedding and MMFF94s optimization). This requirement introduces a severe computational bottleneck, termed conformational overhead, which scales poorly as screening libraries expand into the millions of compounds.

Our empirical evaluation revealed that this added computational cost did not yield commensurate predictive gains. The highly engineered 2D/3D/FP representation achieved an $R^2$ of \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpRTwoMean, which was statistically indistinguishable from the purely structural Morgan fingerprint model ($R^2 = \MorganRTwoMean$, $p = \PairedTTestP$). This parity in performance indicates that the relevant pharmacophoric features governing TgDHFR inhibition are adequately captured by 2D topological connectivity. Consequently, deploying computationally expensive 3D descriptors for this specific target introduces unnecessary dimensionality and processing overhead without improving model fidelity, reinforcing the superiority of lightweight 2D fingerprints for efficient virtual screening pipelines.

\subsection{Methodological Scope, Limitations, and Translational Potential}

The predictive reliability of any quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) model is inherently bounded by the chemical diversity of its training corpus. Given the dataset size of \NCompounds\ compounds, the applicability domain of our optimized ensemble is restricted to the structural scaffolds and physicochemical properties represented within the curated TgDHFR data from ChEMBL and BindingDB. Consequently, extrapolating predictions to highly divergent chemotypes or novel synthetic spaces carries an increased risk of reduced predictive confidence. 

Despite this limitation, the programmatic pipeline developed in this study offers substantial translational utility. By establishing a computationally lightweight, open-source framework, this approach democratizes high-throughput virtual screening. It functions as a highly efficient triage mechanism, rapidly filtering massive libraries of approved drugs to isolate a tractable subset of candidates. This targeted prioritization minimizes the computational expenditure required for subsequent physics-based simulations and reduces the attrition rate in downstream \textit{in vitro} and \textit{in vivo} experimental validations.
