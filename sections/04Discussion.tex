\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Principal findings}
This systematic review and multi-level meta-analysis quantified the current state of artificial intelligence (AI) architectures applied to population-level dengue forecasting, resolving systemic methodological inconsistencies within digital epidemiology. While the pooled analytical evidence confirms that machine learning algorithms provide superior nominal accuracy over traditional baselines ($LRR = \MetaLRR$), their statistical validity is conditioned by study-level factors. The implementation of a robust variance estimation (RVE) framework and the discovery of a high intra-class correlation ($ICC = \MetaICC$) mathematically indicate that performance variance is strongly associated with study-specific data engineering and validation designs, rather than intra-study architectural refinements. Furthermore, a cluster-robust extension of Egger's test ($p = \EggerPval$) suggests publication bias favoring positive outcomes. Nonetheless, comprehensive leave-one-out (LOO) and Cook's distance diagnostic filtering confirmed the structural stability of the global pooled effect size against outlier leverage.

\subsection{Re-evaluating the impact of algorithmic complexity (RQ1 \& RQ3)}
The stratified subgroup analysis confirmed that the predictive superiority of AI remains consistent whether evaluated through continuous error metrics ($LRR = \SubgroupLRRError$) or bounded performance probabilities ($LRR = \SubgroupLRRPerf$). However, our meta-regression successfully disentangled the relative contribution of model design versus data design, revealing a stark divergence in current research paradigms. Crucially, the transition from standard machine learning to deep learning architectures was not associated with a statistically significant shift in relative predictive advantage ($\beta = \MacroCoefisDL$), accounting for a negligible \MacroRsquaredPct\ of the total between-study variance ($R^2 = \MacroRsquared$). Conversely, features related to data curation and variable engineering explained a massive \RegRsquaredPct\ of the performance variance ($R^2 = \RegRsquared$). 

This empirical dichotomy indicates that the current emphasis on developing increasingly complex, multi-layered networks yields diminishing returns relative to data quality. The operational edge in digital epidemiology is largely dictated by the dimensionality and structure of the data matrix, rather than algorithmic sophistication alone. The common practice of treating architectural complexity as a proxy for forecasting efficacy represents a methodological misallocation of resources; engineering robust, high-fidelity feature inputs is far more critical.

This structural insight is further reinforced by the analysis of meteorological and socio-demographic covariates. The cluster-robust meta-regression revealed that incorporating basic meteorological indicators like precipitation ($\beta = \Coefclimprecipitation$) and explicit annual seasonal cycles ($\beta = \Coeftempseasonalitycalendar$) was inversely associated with the relative advantage of AI over historical baselines. Traditional statistical models (e.g., SARIMA) are already highly efficient at capturing predictable seasonal rainfall patterns; supplying these highly linear climate dynamics to a complex neural network adds computational overhead without generating novel predictive signals. 

Instead, the mathematical utility of AI becomes evident when models are exposed to non-linear socio-epidemiological variables---such as severe clinical mortality and case data ($\beta = \Coefepiclinicalmortality$)---which frequently exceed the capacity of classical statistical baselines. However, this capacity is strictly contextual. Our results demonstrate a severe predictive penalty when AI models are deployed in regions characterized by high poverty ($\beta = \Coefsociogdppoverty$, $p \Pvalsociogdppoverty$). This socio-demographic penalty suggests that the highly stochastic transmission dynamics driven by structural inequalities—such as irregular water storage or deficient urban infrastructure—disrupt the pattern recognition capabilities of highly parameterized algorithms, causing them to fail precisely where public health interventions are most critical.

\subsection{Methodological fragility in current AI literature (RQ2 \& RQ5)}
A primary concern identified in this study is the high risk of bias across the literature, driven by validation vulnerabilities that can inflate reported accuracies. Specifically, non-temporal random train-test splits (such as standard $K$-fold cross-validation) persist in \ValRandomSplit\ of the evaluations. Epidemic data is inherently sequential; randomly splitting time-series datasets disrupts the chronological structure, introducing a risk of temporal data leakage where models might utilize future information to predict past events. 

Crucially, our meta-regression illuminated the mathematical penalty of these flawed validation environments. Both random splits ($\beta = \CoefValRandomSplit$) and explicit temporal data leakage risk ($\beta = \CoefLeakageRisk$) exerted a negative dampening effect on the reported comparative effect size. This implies that when advanced AI models and classical baselines are subjected to compromised validation protocols, complex deep learning structures tend to overfit the leaked noise, causing their true out-of-sample predictive advantage to compress. This fragility is compounded by the reliance on simplistic, single-point temporal holdouts (\ValTempHoldout), whereas rigorous dynamic cross-validations, like walk-forward expanding windows (\ValWalkForward), remain underrepresented, and true nested cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning is entirely non-existent (\ValNested).

These methodological factors contextualize the significant reporting asymmetry detected by the cluster-robust Egger's regression test ($p = \EggerPval$). The literature appears conditioned by reporting thresholds where architectures are iterated until they surpass baselines, potentially leading to the systemic under-reporting of less successful configurations. Consequently, when evaluated through the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of evidence supporting the real-world deployment of these models is graded as very low.

\subsection{Addressing the translational gap and digital redundancy (RQ4 \& RQ6)}
Our analysis noted a severe geographical and operational confinement in current modeling practices. Strikingly, none of the evaluated frameworks formally validated their architectures across multiple internal sectors (\GenMultiLoc), and spatial transportability across international borders remained entirely untested (\GenMultiCountry). Furthermore, the integration of novel digital streams revealed significant collinearity issues: digital proxies, such as internet search trends, were systematically penalized or eliminated from the optimized meta-regression due to severe mathematical collinearity (VIF $> 10$). Deep learning models extract latent disease momentum from historical incidence data natively; the inclusion of highly reactive digital search queries frequently exhibited collinear redundancy rather than predictive foresight.

This data redundancy, combined with an absolute lack of automated real-time data pipelines (\GenRealTime), highlights a massive translational gap between technical development and institutional deployment. Through the lens of the Open Science Maturity Index (OSMI), the literature demonstrates a systemic reproducibility crisis. Basic code and dataset accessibility were completely absent (\OsmiCode), meaning advanced reproducibility components---such as environment dependency files (\OsmiEnv), containerized pipelines (\OsmiContainer), and pre-trained weights (\OsmiWeights)---were similarly non-existent. 

This transparency limitation explains the bottleneck identified by the Operational Readiness Index (ORI). While authors frequently report conceptual engagement with health ministries (\OriHealthAuth), this rarely translates into functional infrastructure. Only \OriDashboard\ of studies developed visual interfaces, \OriPipeline\ achieved automated data ingestion, and a mere \OriDecisionMaking\ actively integrated algorithms into public health decision-making. The literature currently prioritizes localized architectural optimization while routinely bypassing the interpretable software engineering, advanced explainability (XAI = \ExpAdvancedXAI), and open science practices fundamentally required for public health integration.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}
A primary strength of this study is its rigorous analytical framework. By implementing a multi-level RVE framework alongside cluster-robust meta-regressions, this review accounted for the extreme hierarchical dependence of multiple model iterations nested within primary studies, circumventing the statistical inflation common in conventional meta-analyses. The use of the log response ratio (LRR) facilitated the harmonization of heterogeneous performance metrics without sacrificing continuous error dispersion or bounded classification scales. 

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As with any meta-regression, these findings represent statistical associations across study-level aggregates, not causal mechanistic proofs. The identified covariates highlight correlational patterns in model performance but require prospective, controlled validation. Additionally, the quantitative synthesis was bounded by the reporting quality of the primary literature. The widespread omission of explicit sample sizes, exact baseline comparators, and variance metadata necessitated a high attrition rate during global effect pooling. Furthermore, harmonizing these fragmentary metrics required statistical assumptions regarding variance imputation (e.g., standardizing the coefficient of variation). While this imputation may marginally influence the width of the estimated confidence intervals, our comprehensive diagnostic filtering mathematically confirms the directional stability of the pooled effects.
