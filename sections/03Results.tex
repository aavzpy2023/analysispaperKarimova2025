\section{Results}
\subsection{Study selection and characteristics}
The initial qualitative database search and screening phase identified \RqTotalN\ unique empirical articles forecasting dengue incidence. However, the progression to the quantitative meta-analysis highlighted substantial methodological inconsistencies across the current literature. A comprehensive data extraction process yielded an initial matrix of \TotalExtractedModels\ granular model iterations. 

To prevent statistical inflation and preserve the integrity of variance weighting, the data were rigorously filtered. Rather than expanding the matrix with unverified configurations, both the global effect pooling (Forest Plot) and the cluster-robust meta-regressions strictly utilized the exact same stratified dataset. Only paired champion-versus-baseline iterations possessing explicit temporal sample sizes and necessary variance metadata were retained, resulting in a definitive analytical matrix of $N = \TotalValidModels$ comparative evaluations. The high attrition rate during this primary pooling underscores a critical reproducibility deficit in the landscape of AI-driven dengue forecasting, as the majority of extracted iterations failed to formally benchmark their novel architectures against a parsimonious baseline or completely omitted fundamental statistical metadata.

The restriction of the cluster-robust meta-regression to the exact same analytical subset used for global effect pooling ($N = \TotalValidModels$) represents a deliberate methodological decision to ensure absolute analytical synchronization. While multi-level meta-regressions can theoretically accommodate expanded matrices with missing baseline links, restricting the regression to high-fidelity, fully-characterized paired iterations prevents the introduction of unweighted noise or residual confounding into the Weighted Least Squares (WLS) framework. Consequently, the estimated socio-epidemiological and algorithmic covariates directly account for the true statistical heterogeneity ($I^2$) quantified in the primary pooling, preserving the mathematical internal validity of the explanatory architecture.

\subsection{Architectural sophistication versus feature engineering impact (RQ1)}
To evaluate the predictive capacity of AI against traditional baselines without confounding distinct metric scales, the primary quantitative synthesis was stratified across divergent metric families using a robust variance estimation (RVE) framework to preserve statistical independence across nested study clusters ($k = \MetaK{}$) \cite{lowe2017}. When isolating continuous, unbounded error metrics (e.g., RMSE, MAE; $k = \SubgroupKError$), the predictive superiority of AI remained consistent ($LRR = \SubgroupLRRError$). This advantage was directionally consistent and statistically equivalent when evaluating bounded classification and performance metrics independently (e.g., AUC, $R^2$; $k = \SubgroupKPerf$, $LRR = \SubgroupLRRPerf$). The consistent algorithmic advantage across distinct mathematical dimensions demonstrates that the observed AI superiority is a genuine phenomenon rather than an artifact of metric aggregation.

Consolidating these families into a global random-effects model yielded a positive global effect size of $LRR = \MetaLRR$ (95\% CI: \MetaCILow\ to \MetaCIHigh, $p = \MetaPval$) \cite{Guo2017}. This pooled effect indicates a substantial nominal improvement in forecasting accuracy over classical epidemiological baselines.

The variance imputation sensitivity analysis confirmed the structural stability of the AI advantage: the pooled LRR remained stable across a theoretical spectrum of assumed dispersion, ranging from highly constrained ($CV = 0.5$, $LRR = \MetaLRRcvZeroFive$) to highly dispersed ($CV = 2.0$, $LRR = \MetaLRRcvTwoZero$) assumptions. This algorithmic stability was further validated via iterative leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity tests, demonstrating that the global LRR remained consistently positive regardless of single-study omissions (range: \LooMinLRR\ to \LooMaxLRR). Figure \ref{fig:forest_plot} illustrates these global findings.

To dissect the algorithmic hierarchy among the extracted models, we conducted a macro-level weighted least squares (WLS) regression comparing explicitly defined deep learning (DL) architectures against traditional machine learning ensembles \cite{anggraeni2021, Zhao2023}. Interestingly, the results demonstrated a nominal, but not statistically significant, predictive advantage for deep learning frameworks over traditional ML ($\beta = \MacroCoefisDL$, $p = \MacroPvalisDL$), explaining only \MacroRsquaredPct\ of the total between-study variance ($R^2 = \MacroRsquared$) \cite{liz2022}. To ensure this algorithmic assessment was not obscured by statistical anomalies, a sensitivity analysis was executed using Cook's distance influence diagnostics. The model identified \CooksOutliers\ influential leverage points ($D > 4/n$). Recalculating the cluster-robust WLS regression on this restricted subset confirmed the structural findings ($R^2 = \SensRsquared$, $p = \SensPvalisDL$), indicating that while the DL contribution marginally increases when removing outlier leverage, the raw architectural complexity remains a weak driver of overall performance.

Contrasting these architectural gains against data design choices highlights a fundamental structural insight. While the internal algorithmic architecture accounts for a mere \MacroRsquaredPct\ of performance variance, our optimized multi-level predictive model demonstrates that data engineering explains a massive \RegRsquaredPct\ of the variance in AI superiority ($R^2 = \RegRsquared$, $p \RegPval$). This robust finding strongly suggests that relative performance gains across the digital epidemiology landscape are primarily driven by strategic feature selection rather than raw architectural complexity.

\subsection{Assessment of publication bias and evidence robustness (RQ2)}
While the global pooling indicates a significant advantage for AI frameworks, it also revealed high statistical dispersion. Cochran's Q test ($Q = \MetaQStat$, $p \MetaQPval$) and the random-effects weights indicated substantial between-study heterogeneity ($I^2 = \MetaIsq$, $\tau^2_{PM} = \MetaTauPM$). In digital epidemiology, this dispersion is an expected statistical characteristic reflecting systemic variations in geographic targets, temporal scales, and validation architectures \cite{lowe2017, cheng20201, Akshaiya2018}. 

To evaluate the statistical validity of this evidence base, the hierarchical dependence of the dataset was quantified via the intra-class correlation coefficient ($ICC \MetaICC$). As established by the variance components boundary effect, this near-perfect study-level clustering confirms that performance variance is driven predominantly by study-specific methodological designs (e.g., localized data curation and validation choices) rather than intra-study iterations, mathematically justifying our multi-level RVE framework. Under this nested architecture, the visual asymmetry of the funnel plot (Figure \ref{fig:funnel_plot}) was evaluated using a cluster-robust extension of Egger's linear regression test ($p = \EggerPval$). This result suggests a pronounced propensity for small-study effects and selective reporting, indicating that the literature may be conditioned by publication thresholds where architectures failing to outperform historical benchmarks are systematically under-reported.

To synthesize the overall reliability of this evidence base, we applied the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework adapted for predictive modeling. The initial assumption of high-quality evidence was systematically downgraded based on quantitative findings. Certainty was downgraded by two levels due to a high risk of bias, evidenced by the PROBAST evaluation and the presence of time leakage in \MethTimeLeakage\ of studies. It was downgraded by one level for inconsistency, driven by the unexplained heterogeneity ($I^2 = \MetaIsq$). A downgrade for indirectness was applied due to a lack of external validation (\MethExtVal) and prospective forecasting (\MethProspForecast), indicating that current models often represent localized epidemiological niches rather than generalizable early warning systems. Finally, evidence was downgraded for publication bias due to the significant asymmetry detected by Egger's test ($p = \EggerPval$). Consequently, the overall certainty of evidence supporting the real-world operational deployment of these models is graded as very low.

\subsection{The climate paradox and meteorological covariates (RQ3)}
The robust cluster meta-regression quantified the impact of meteorological covariates. While previous literature assumes weather data consistently drives AI performance, our results revealed a negative coefficient for both precipitation ($\beta = \Coefclimprecipitation$, $p \Pvalclimprecipitation$) and explicit calendar seasonality ($\beta = \Coeftempseasonalitycalendar$, $p \Pvaltempseasonalitycalendar$). This indicates that classical baselines (e.g., SARIMA) are already highly efficient at modeling predictable seasonal rainfall and annual cycles; therefore, supplying explicit seasonality to an AI reduces its relative predictive advantage over these parsimonious models. 

Crucially, the cluster-robust regression exposed a stark socio-demographic penalty. Poverty emerged as a statistically significant negative predictor of relative AI performance ($\beta = \Coefsociogdppoverty$, $p \Pvalsociogdppoverty$). This indicates that when models are deployed in contexts of high socioeconomic vulnerability, the predictive superiority of advanced AI frameworks is severely attenuated. This attenuation likely occurs because vulnerable populations exhibit highly non-linear, stochastic transmission dynamics driven by structural inequalities—such as irregular water storage or deficient urban infrastructure—causing highly parameterized algorithms to struggle precisely where they are needed most. Rather than predictable climate fluctuations, the predictive superiority of AI was primarily driven by its capacity to process complex clinical shocks, specifically clinical mortality and severe case data ($\beta = \Coefepiclinicalmortality$, $p \Pvalepiclinicalmortality$).

\subsection{The mathematical penalty of validation bias}
Furthermore, the meta-regression illuminated the pervasive impact of validation bias on reported outcomes. Previously obscured by statistical noise, the extraction of rigorous methodological covariates revealed that validation shortcuts directly distort perceived algorithmic superiority. The implementation of naive random temporal splits ($\beta = \CoefValRandomSplit$, $p \PvalValRandomSplit$) and architectures exhibiting a high risk of data leakage ($\beta = \CoefLeakageRisk$, $p \PvalLeakageRisk$) exerted a negative penalty on the comparative effect size. 

These negative coefficients indicate that when classical baselines and AI models are subjected to flawed validation environments (e.g., tuning hyperparameters on test sets), the complex AI structures tend to overfit disastrously, causing their out-of-sample advantage to collapse under robust scrutiny. This mathematical penalty confirms that rigorous validation design (e.g., strictly partitioned walk-forward forecasting) is not merely a theoretical best practice, but a fundamental determinant of actual translational viability.

\subsection{Data deserts and the value of digital redundancy (RQ4)}
An evaluation of feature usage frequency revealed notable data sparsity: while historical local cases (\Freqepiincidencerate) and basic temperature (\Freqclimtemperature) dominated feature engineering, socio-spatial determinants such as human mobility (\Freqsociomobility) and urban infrastructure (\Freqsociourbaninfra) were rarely utilized. 

To isolate the impact of specific covariates, we conducted a cluster-robust OLS meta-regression across $N=\TotalRegModels$ algorithmic evaluations. To prevent multicollinearity, an iterative variance inflation factor (VIF) filter (threshold $< 10.0$) was applied. This rigorous filtering process revealed that digital search trends (e.g., Google/Social Media) often exhibit high collinearity with established epidemiological curves and were consequently penalized or excluded by the VIF threshold. This mathematically demonstrates that the predictive signal provided by internet search queries is highly redundant when robust epidemiological and eco-climatic features are already present in the model. Deep learning algorithms naturally infer disease momentum from historical incidence rates; forcing explicit digital proxies often introduces noise rather than genuine predictive enhancement.

\subsection{Temporal scalability and validation fragility (RQ5)}
A rigorous quality evaluation was conducted using the PROBAST framework across four domains: participants/data sources, predictors, outcomes, and analysis. The results expose methodological fragility across the field (Figure \ref{fig:risk_of_bias}).

An audit of cross-validation architectures exposed limitations in model generalizability. In digital epidemiology, chronological integrity is critical; however, data leakage remains a significant concern in the \textit{analysis} domain. Despite the chronological structure of epidemic series, non-temporal random splits (such as standard K-fold cross-validation) persist in \ValRandomSplit\ of the evaluations. The cluster-robust meta-regression revealed that this methodological shortcut exerts a nominal negative penalty on the comparative effect size ($\beta = \CoefValRandomSplit$, $p \PvalValRandomSplit$). Similarly, isolating studies with explicit temporal leakage risk demonstrated a negative dampening effect on reported performance, approaching marginal significance ($\beta = \CoefLeakageRisk$, $p \PvalLeakageRisk$). While not crossing the strict $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold, the negative directionality of these coefficients confirms that when AI architectures are subjected to rigorous, uncompromised validation environments, their out-of-sample advantage tends to compress rather than inflate.

The literature relies heavily on basic validation setups: \ValTempHoldout\ of evaluations utilized a single-point temporal holdout. Dynamic evaluations were severely underrepresented, with only \ValWalkForward\ employing time-series expanding windows (walk-forward validation) and \ValRolling\ applying fixed rolling windows. True nested cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning was entirely absent (\ValNested). 

Furthermore, true prospective forecasting was executed in only \MethProspForecast\ of the studies. Architectures subjected to strict external validation (\MethExtVal) maintained comparable effect sizes against their baselines ($\beta = \CoefExtValidation$, $p \PvalExtValidation$). When analyzing temporal scalability, \MethHorizShort\ focused on short-term horizons ($< 1$ week), \MethHorizMed\ on medium-term horizons (up to 1 month), and \MethHorizLong\ targeted long-term forecasts ($> 1$ month).

\subsection{The translational gap: operational readiness and open science (RQ6)}
The translational gap in AI forecasting is driven by severe limitations in spatial scalability and open-source availability. Tellingly, none of the extracted studies evaluated their architectures across multiple locations within the same region (\GenMultiLoc) or verified spatial generalizability across different countries (\GenMultiCountry). Furthermore, true real-time automated forecasting capabilities were entirely absent (\GenRealTime) from the evaluated frameworks.

Regarding model interpretability, while \ExpFeatureImp\ of studies reported basic feature importance, only \ExpAdvancedXAI\ utilized advanced explainable AI (XAI) frameworks to interpret non-linear predictions, such as SHAP (\ExpSHAP) or LIME (\ExpLIME).

The Open Science Maturity Index (OSMI) highlighted a profound reproducibility deficit. At Level 1 (Availability), the release of open-source code repositories and datasets was completely absent (\OsmiCode). Consequently, at Level 2 (Reproducibility), the provision of environment dependency files (\OsmiEnv), containerized architectures (\OsmiContainer), and pre-trained model weights (\OsmiWeights) were universally absent, severely limiting the replicability and external auditing of current machine learning frameworks.

The Operational Readiness Index (ORI) indicated a massive gap between technical development and institutional adoption. While \OriHealthAuth\ of studies reported some level of conceptual engagement with health authorities, only \OriDashboard\ developed functional web dashboards, and \OriPipeline\ implemented automated data ingestion pipelines. Consequently, a mere \OriDecisionMaking\ of the proposed algorithms were actively integrated into real-world public health decision-making. The literature currently emphasizes localized, isolated architectural optimization while largely neglecting the interpretable software engineering pipelines required for actual public health implementation.
