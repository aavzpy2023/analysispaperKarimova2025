\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Bioactivity Benchmark Dataset and Curation}

The dataset used in this study was derived from a previously published benchmark on \textit{Toxoplasma gondii} dihydrofolate reductase (TgDHFR) enzyme inhibitors \cite{karimova2025}. The provenance of these data relies on the integration of primary public repositories to ensure chemical and bioactivity representativeness, aligning with foundational QSAR guidelines that mandate rigorous data preparation \cite{tropsha2010}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Primary Data Sources:} The initial collection was constructed by combining records from BindingDB, which provided detailed drug-protein interaction parameters and SMILES structures, alongside bioactivity data retrieved from ChEMBL \cite{karimova2025}.
	\item \textbf{Consolidation and Curation Protocol:} Following database integration, discordant duplicates and intermediate compounds were systematically removed to mitigate experimental noise and avoid overfitting, adhering to internationally recognized QSAR curation standards \cite{cherkasov2014, iwuala2025}. Automated standardization steps, such as salt stripping and structural validation, were applied to guarantee overall chemical integrity \cite{damilola2025}.
	\item \textbf{Treatment of Experimental Variations:} To address compounds with multiple $IC_{50}$ measurements obtained under varying conditions, the Interquartile Range (IQR) method was employed to filter out statistical outliers across identical CID identifiers \cite{karimova2025}. For the remaining consistent entries, an average $IC_{50}$ value was calculated to establish a single representative measurement per ligand group \cite{karimova2025}.
	\item \textbf{Target Variable Formulation:} The final dataset comprises a benchmark of 873 unique molecules \cite{karimova2025}. The continuous target variable was formulated as $pIC_{50}$, defined as the negative logarithm of the molar $IC_{50}$ concentration ($pIC_{50} = -\log_{10}(IC_{50})$) \cite{karimova2025}. This logarithmic conversion standardizes the response values, reduces variance, and compresses the active range, rendering it optimal for robust regression algorithms \cite{karimova2025}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Representation Spaces: Molecular Fingerprints and Conformational Descriptors}

To systematically evaluate the structure-activity relationship, three distinct molecular representation spaces were programmatically generated using the open-source cheminformatics library RDKit \cite{landrum2025}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Molecular Fingerprints (ECFP4):} Morgan fingerprints were computed to represent molecular structures as vector spaces \cite{samina2019}. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) were specifically developed for structure-activity modeling because they can be calculated rapidly and represent an essentially infinite number of distinct molecular features, including stereochemical information \cite{rogers2010}. Furthermore, classical 2D fingerprint-based models have been demonstrated to perform as well as state-of-the-art 3D structure-based models for bioactivity predictions relying solely on ligand information \cite{gao2020}.
	\item \textbf{2D Physicochemical Descriptors:} A comprehensive set of conventional two-dimensional topological representations was extracted to establish a quantitative physicochemical baseline \cite{samina2019}. Well-curated 2D molecular representations are highly robust, and recent studies have shown they can maintain scaffold-robust advantages over 3D alternatives in various permeability and property prediction tasks \cite{tiwari2026}.
	\item \textbf{3D Conformational Descriptors (ETKDG):} Three-dimensional conformers were generated employing the distance geometry approach implemented in RDKit \cite{riniker2015}. Because purely distance-based constraints tend to lead to distorted aromatic rings and $sp^2$ centers, this methodology utilizes experimental torsion-angle preferences obtained from small-molecule crystallographic data to improve conformation generation \cite{riniker2015}. Although recent evaluations indicate that 3D conformer descriptors may provide limited incremental predictive value over curated 2D representations in specific tabular benchmark algorithms \cite{tiwari2026}, integrating spatial information remains highly relevant for molecular image-based modeling \cite{matsu2023} and geometry-augmented representation learning \cite{yanan2024}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Strict 15-Fold Nested Cross-Validation Protocol}

In a standard cross-validation protocol, the dataset is partitioned into $k$ disjoint blocks that are systematically rotated between training and testing phases \cite{calle2025, krstajic2014}. While conventional nested cross-validation (NCV) literature frequently employs symmetrical $5 \times 5$ or $10 \times 10$ architectures \cite{calle2025, lewis2023, wainer2021}, there is no methodological restriction requiring identical fold counts for the inner and outer loops \cite{krstajic2014}. To guarantee leak-free evaluation, balance computational cost, and mitigate model selection bias in our low-data regime ($N = 873$), a strict $15 \times 5$ asymmetric NCV protocol was implemented. This architecture mathematically decouples model assessment from model selection using two distinct concentric loops:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Outer Loop (15-Fold Generalization Assessment):} To achieve a lower variance in performance estimation compared to extreme approaches like Leave-One-Out (LOO), the dataset was partitioned into 15 independent folds \cite{krstajic2014}. In each iteration, one fold is strictly held out as the test set while the remaining 14 folds form the training pool. This ensures the test data is never used to optimize model performance, yielding an unbiased out-of-sample prediction estimate \cite{varma2006, wainer2021}.
	\item \textbf{Inner Loop (5-Fold Optimization and Feature Selection):} Within each 14-fold training pool, a secondary 5-fold inner loop was embedded to execute Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (\texttt{Optuna}) and feature selection. Confining these steps entirely within the inner partitions forces the selection of more parsimonious models and prevents inadvertent information leakage from the test set—a critical requirement since filtering predictors on the whole dataset produces severely overoptimistic performance metrics \cite{calle2025, lewis2023, parvandeh2020}.
\end{itemize}

Three distinct machine learning algorithms—Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM)—were evaluated under this framework. Although computationally intensive, prioritizing this custom nested configuration over a flat cross-validation approach is methodologically mandatory to achieve realistic and robust $pIC_{50}$ predictive estimates \cite{wainer2021, krstajic2014}.

\subsection{Deep Learning Baseline: Directed Message Passing Neural Networks}

To benchmark the classical machine learning models against state-of-the-art representation learning, a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) was implemented using the open-source Chemprop framework (version 2.0) in Python \cite{graff2026, yang2019}. The baseline was executed by allocating a 15\% hold-out test set (\texttt{TEST\_SIZE = 0.15}) with a fixed random state (\texttt{RANDOM\_STATE = 42}) to ensure reproducibility and strict alignment with the classical stacking ensembles.

The deep learning architecture and training pipeline were explicitly structured as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Graph Construction and Initial Featurization:} Molecular SMILES strings were transformed into graph representations using RDKit, where atoms serve as vertices and bonds as edges \cite{heid2024}. Specifically, the \texttt{SimpleMoleculeMolGraphFeaturizer()} was utilized to construct initial feature vectors based on atomic identity and topological connectivity.
	\item \textbf{Directed Message Passing and Aggregation:} The network processes the graphs using \texttt{BondMessagePassing()} to learn embeddings directly from directed edges rather than vertices. This design prevents spurious feedback loops (totters), significantly reducing noise in the learned representation \cite{yang2019}. These atomic representations were subsequently pooled into a unified molecular vector via a \texttt{MeanAggregation()} function.
	\item \textbf{Target Normalization and FFN Projection:} Prior to training, the continuous $pIC_{50}$ targets were scaled using internal data normalization. The aggregated embeddings were then passed through a \texttt{RegressionFFN()} equipped with batch normalization (\texttt{batch\_norm=True}) to generate the final predictive output.
	\item \textbf{Training and Evaluation Protocol:} Model weights were optimized via PyTorch Lightning over a maximum of 100 epochs. The internal evaluation metric configured during training was Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Out-of-sample performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE), augmented by a 95\% confidence interval derived from 2,000 bootstrap iterations (\texttt{N\_BOOTSTRAP = 2000}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Virtual Screening Protocol and Ligand Efficiency Profiling}

An automated virtual screening pipeline was deployed to evaluate an FDA-approved compound library (sourced from DrugBank), integrating predicted bioactivities with molecular efficiency metrics and pharmacokinetic profiling. To systematically prioritize the most viable lead candidates, the evaluation was structured across four quantitative filtering stages:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Binding Affinity and Safety Thresholds:} To identify potent target inhibitors while mitigating off-target liabilities, strict affinity criteria were enforced. A binding affinity threshold of $-9.54\text{ kcal/mol}$ (equivalent to $K_i = 0.1\text{ }\mu\text{M}$) was applied to select high-affinity compounds, while a baseline cutoff of $-8.18\text{ kcal/mol}$ ($K_i = 1\text{ }\mu\text{M}$) was utilized to screen out molecules with potential adverse side effects, such as hERG potassium channel blockade \cite{feng2023}.
	\item \textbf{Ligand Efficiency (LE) and Binding Efficiency Index (BEI):} Candidate prioritization deliberately avoided relying exclusively on raw potency to prevent "molecular obesity" during high-throughput screening follow-ups \cite{hopkins2004}. Instead, compounds were evaluated using dual efficiency metrics. Candidates were required to exceed the established threshold of $\text{LE} > 0.3\text{ kcal/mol}$ per heavy atom \cite{hopkins2014}. Additionally, the Binding Efficiency Index (BEI) was incorporated to substitute heavy atom count ($\text{HA}$) with total molecular weight ($\text{MW}$ in $\text{kDa}$), effectively correcting \textit{in vitro} activity for physicochemical property load and better accounting for heteroatom contributions \cite{abad2005, hopkins2014}. The BEI was computed as:
	\begin{equation}
		\text{BEI} = \frac{pIC_{50}}{\text{MW}_{\text{kDa}}}
	\end{equation}
	where a reference target of approximately $27.0$ represents an idealized $1\text{ nM}$ potency ($pIC_{50} = 9.00$) at $0.333\text{ kDa}$ \cite{abad2005}. While composite indices dividing logarithmic values by linear parameters possess known mathematical limitations regarding size dependency \cite{shultz2013}, they remain established, pragmatic tools to guide molecular optimization.
	\item \textbf{Lipophilic Ligand Efficiency (LLE):} To effectively balance potency against lipophilicity and mitigate the risk of nonspecific binding, LLE was integrated into the scoring function. Only compounds maintaining an $\text{LLE} > 5$ (derived from $\text{cLogP}$ and binding constants) were prioritized, ensuring favorable physicochemical space \cite{hopkins2014}.
	\item \textbf{Comprehensive ADMET Profiling:} The virtual screening protocol concluded with a predictive pharmacokinetic assessment encompassing absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET). Because a drug candidate with satisfactory therapeutic efficacy must also meet appropriate pharmacokinetic criteria, this final ADMET profiling acts as a critical filter to significantly reduce the risk of late-stage failure in drug design \cite{feng2023}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Orthogonal Physics-Based Validation via Molecular Docking}

To complement ligand-based machine learning predictions, an orthogonal structure-based evaluation using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 is introduced \cite{rajesh2026, feng2023, trott2009, eberhardt2021}. This physical validation translates abstract chemical representations into spatial coordinates, mitigating the risk of false positives from scaffold memorization by ensuring genuine structural complementarity \cite{yang2019, rajesh2026}. Vina employs an Iterated Local Search optimizer and a hybrid scoring function, calibrated against PDBbind data, to guide flexible ligands into optimal thermodynamic conformations \cite{trott2009, eberhardt2021}.

Three-dimensional receptor coordinates are retrieved from the Protein Data Bank \cite{consortium2019, berman2003} and prepared using standard computational protocols \cite{damilola2025, berman2002}. Following the removal of crystallographic water and local minimization, polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges are assigned to generate the required PDBQT files \cite{trott2009, damilola2025}. A 3D grid box is then centered on the active site, carefully dimensioned to allow unrestricted exploration of ligand orientations and flexible rotatable bonds, thereby preventing crystal-pose centering bias \cite{trott2009, damilola2025}.

Vina evaluates and ranks the docked poses based on the predicted Gibbs free energy of binding ($\Delta G$) in kcal/mol, where lower energies denote stronger thermodynamic stability \cite{trott2009, feng2023}. Strict potency thresholds are applied to isolate highly potent candidates \cite{feng2023, hopkins2014}. Finally, this physics-based energy is normalized against the heavy atom count to calculate Ligand Efficiency (LE), prioritizing compounds with LE $> 0.3$ kcal/mol per heavy atom to ensure the selected inhibitors reside within favorable, non-obese drug-like chemical space \cite{shultz2013, hopkins2014, abad2005}.

\subsection{Computational Environment and Reproducibility}

To ensure full algorithmic transparency and computational reproducibility, all data curation, model training, and evaluation pipelines were programmatically implemented in Python (version 3.14.7). The computational environment was standardized using the following core software stack:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Cheminformatics and Featurization:} Molecular parsing, standardization, and descriptor generation (2D/3D/ECFP4) were executed using RDKit (version 2025.9.3).
	\item \textbf{Classical Machine Learning:} The nested cross-validation framework, hyperparameter Bayesian optimization, and baseline algorithms were built utilizing \texttt{scikit-learn} (version 1.8.0), \texttt{xgboost} (version 3.1.2), \texttt{lightgbm} (version 4.6.0), and \texttt{optuna}. Data handling was managed via \texttt{pandas} (version 2.3.3) and \texttt{numpy} (version 2.3.5).
	\item \textbf{Deep Learning Infrastructure:} The D-MPNN graph models were trained using \texttt{chemprop} (version 2.0) and \texttt{lightning}, with tensor operations supported by PyTorch (\texttt{torch} $\geq$ 2.0.0). 
	\item \textbf{Hardware Specifications:} Model training and evaluation were performed on a Ubuntu 26.04 LTS workstation equipped with Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2695 and utilizing CPU-only acceleration.
\end{itemize}
