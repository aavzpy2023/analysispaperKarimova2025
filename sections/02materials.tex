\subsection{Bioactivity Benchmark Dataset and Curation}

The dataset used in this study was derived from a previously published benchmark on \textit{Toxoplasma gondii} dihydrofolate reductase (TgDHFR) enzyme inhibitors \cite{karimova2025}. The provenance of these data relies on the integration of primary public repositories to ensure chemical and bioactivity representativeness, aligning with foundational QSAR guidelines that mandate rigorous data preparation \cite{tropsha2010}. By utilizing the exact baseline dataset compiled by Karimova and Senanayake \cite{karimova2025}, we establish a strict control framework. This methodological choice allows us to isolate and evaluate the true predictive power of classical machine learning algorithms against computationally heavy statistical techniques, deliberately omitting synthetic data augmentation to assess performance strictly on empirical ground-truth data.

The initial data collection was constructed by combining records from BindingDB, which provided detailed drug-protein interaction parameters and SMILES structures, alongside bioactivity data retrieved from ChEMBL \cite{karimova2025}. Following database integration, automated standardization steps---such as salt stripping and structural validation---were applied to guarantee overall chemical integrity \cite{damilola2025}. Crucially, of the initial 873 entries retrieved, 117 compounds were systematically excluded during this phase to mitigate experimental noise and avoid overfitting \cite{cherkasov2014, iwuala2025}. This specific exclusion was driven by two structural criteria: the presence of organometallic complexes that are fundamentally incompatible with subsequent 3D force-field generation, and the existence of undefined stereocenters that introduce unacceptable conformational ambiguity into the modeling pipeline.

To address the remaining compounds with multiple $IC_{50}$ measurements obtained under varying experimental conditions, the Interquartile Range (IQR) method was employed. Specifically, duplicate assays exhibiting a bioactivity variance exceeding 1.5 log units were filtered out as statistical outliers \cite{karimova2025}. For the remaining consistent entries, an average $IC_{50}$ value was calculated to establish a single, reliable representative measurement per ligand group. 

This rigorous curation protocol yielded a final, highly curated benchmark of $N = 756$ unique small molecules. The continuous target variable was formulated as $pIC_{50}$, defined as the negative logarithm of the molar $IC_{50}$ concentration ($pIC_{50} = -\log_{10}(IC_{50})$) \cite{karimova2025}. This logarithmic conversion standardizes the response values, reduces variance, and compresses the active range, rendering the target variable optimal for robust regression algorithms.

\subsection{Representation Spaces: Molecular Fingerprints and Conformational Descriptors}

To systematically evaluate the structure-activity relationship, three distinct molecular representation spaces were programmatically generated using the open-source cheminformatics library RDKit \cite{landrum2025}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Molecular Fingerprints (ECFP4):} Morgan fingerprints were computed to represent molecular structures as vector spaces \cite{samina2019}. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) were specifically developed for structure-activity modeling because they can be calculated rapidly and represent an essentially infinite number of distinct molecular features, including stereochemical information \cite{rogers2010}. Furthermore, classical 2D fingerprint-based models have been demonstrated to perform as well as state-of-the-art 3D structure-based models for bioactivity predictions relying solely on ligand information \cite{gao2020}.
	\item \textbf{2D Physicochemical Descriptors:} A comprehensive set of conventional two-dimensional topological representations was extracted to establish a quantitative physicochemical baseline \cite{samina2019}. Well-curated 2D molecular representations are highly robust, and recent studies have shown they can maintain scaffold-robust advantages over 3D alternatives in various permeability and property prediction tasks \cite{tiwari2026}.
	\item \textbf{3D Conformational Descriptors (ETKDG):} Three-dimensional conformers were generated employing the distance geometry approach implemented in RDKit \cite{riniker2015}. Because purely distance-based constraints tend to lead to distorted aromatic rings and $sp^2$ centers, this methodology utilizes experimental torsion-angle preferences obtained from small-molecule crystallographic data to improve conformation generation \cite{riniker2015}. Although recent evaluations indicate that 3D conformer descriptors may provide limited incremental predictive value over curated 2D representations in specific tabular benchmark algorithms \cite{tiwari2026}, integrating spatial information remains highly relevant for molecular image-based modeling \cite{matsu2023} and geometry-augmented representation learning \cite{yanan2024}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Strict 15-Fold Nested Cross-Validation Protocol}

In a standard cross-validation protocol, the dataset is partitioned into $k$ disjoint blocks that are systematically rotated between training and testing phases \cite{calle2025, krstajic2014}. While conventional nested cross-validation (NCV) literature frequently employs symmetrical $5 \times 5$ or $10 \times 10$ architectures \cite{calle2025, lewis2023, wainer2021}, there is no methodological restriction requiring identical fold counts for the inner and outer loops \cite{krstajic2014}. To guarantee leak-free evaluation, balance computational cost, and mitigate model selection bias in this severe low-data regime ($N = 756$), a strict $15 \times 5$ asymmetric NCV protocol was implemented. This architecture mathematically decouples model assessment from model selection using two distinct concentric loops.

The choice of data partitioning strategy is critical in small-scale QSAR modeling \cite{kyrali2022}. While Bemis-Murcko scaffold splitting is widely advocated to test out-of-domain extrapolation and prevent structural leakage between folds \cite{yang2019, aga2025, beatrice2026},  a random nested cross-validation scheme was deliberately selected for this study. In a highly restricted dataset of 756 compounds, scaffold splitting frequently generates severely imbalanced folds, creating artificial ``activity cliffs" where entire bioactivity ranges are absent from the training pool. This instability prevents the inner optimization loop from converging on generalizable hyperparameters. Because the primary objective of this model is interpolation within the established applicability domain of known TgDHFR inhibitors—rather than extrapolation to entirely novel chemotypes—random splitting ensures that the bioactivity distribution remains statistically consistent across all folds, enabling robust and stable hyperparameter tuning.

The outer loop of the NCV framework was designed for generalization assessment. To achieve a lower variance in performance estimation compared to extreme approaches like Leave-One-Out (LOO) \cite{krstajic2014}, the dataset was partitioned into 15 independent folds . In each iteration, one fold is strictly held out as the test set while the remaining 14 folds form the training pool. This strict isolation ensures that the test data is never exposed to the model during the optimization phase, yielding a truly unbiased out-of-sample prediction estimate \cite{varma2006, wainer2021}.

Conversely, the inner loop was dedicated exclusively to optimization and feature selection. Within each 14-fold training pool, a secondary 5-fold inner loop was embedded to execute Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (via \texttt{Optuna}) and feature selection. Confining these steps entirely within the inner partitions forces the selection of more parsimonious models and prevents inadvertent information leakage from the test set. This encapsulation is a critical methodological requirement, as filtering predictors or tuning parameters across the entire dataset inevitably produces severely overoptimistic performance metrics \cite{parvandeh2020, lewis2023, calle2025}.

Three distinct machine learning algorithms—Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM)—were evaluated under this framework. Although computationally intensive, prioritizing this custom nested configuration over a flat cross-validation approach is methodologically mandatory to achieve realistic and robust $pIC_{50}$ predictive estimates \cite{wainer2021, krstajic2014}.

\subsection{Deep Learning Baseline: Directed Message Passing Neural Networks}

To benchmark the classical machine learning models against advanced representation learning, a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) was implemented using the open-source Chemprop framework (version 2.0) in Python \cite{graff2026, yang2019}. While the classical ensembles were evaluated using a computationally intensive 15-fold nested cross-validation protocol, executing a comparable nested framework for deep learning architectures is often computationally prohibitive and highly susceptible to overfitting on small datasets ($N = 756$). Consequently, the D-MPNN baseline was executed using a static 15\% hold-out test set. To ensure a rigorous and fair comparison, this partition was generated using a fixed random state (\texttt{RANDOM\_STATE = 42}), strictly aligning the test distribution with the validation protocols established in the original study and our classical baselines.

The deep learning architecture processes molecular SMILES strings by transforming them into graph representations using RDKit, where atoms serve as vertices and bonds as edges \cite{heid2024}. The \texttt{SimpleMolecule\allowbreak MolGraph\allowbreak Featurizer()} constructed initial feature vectors based on atomic identity and topological connectivity. Subsequently, the network applied \texttt{BondMessage\allowbreak Passing()} to learn embeddings directly from directed edges rather than vertices. This design prevents spurious feedback loops (totters), significantly reducing noise in the learned representation \cite{yang2019}. These atomic representations were then pooled into a unified molecular vector via a \texttt{Mean\allowbreak Aggregation()} function.

Prior to training, the continuous $pIC_{50}$ targets underwent internal data normalization. The aggregated embeddings were passed through a \texttt{Regression\allowbreak FFN()} equipped with batch normalization to generate the final predictive output. Model weights were optimized via PyTorch Lightning over a maximum of 100 epochs. This epoch limit, alongside default Chemprop hyperparameters (e.g., hidden size, depth, and learning rate), was deliberately selected to represent a robust baseline. Extensive hyperparameter tuning for deep learning models on datasets of this size frequently induces severe overfitting rather than genuine performance gains \cite{cawley2010, hadas2023}. Therefore, evaluating the network under standard configurations ensures a fair comparison against the baseline capabilities of classical ensembles.

The internal evaluation metric configured during training was Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Out-of-sample performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). To compensate for the variance estimation typically provided by cross-validation, the D-MPNN performance metrics were augmented by a 95\% confidence interval derived from 2,000 bootstrap iterations on the hold-out predictions. This approach yielded a final test performance of $R^2 = \ChemPropDMPNNRTwoMean$ (95\% CI [\ChemPropDMPNNCILow, \ChemPropDMPNNCIHigh]), providing a statistically bounded benchmark against the classical machine learning architectures.

\subsection{Virtual Screening Protocol and Ligand Efficiency Profiling}

An automated virtual screening pipeline was deployed to evaluate an FDA-approved compound library sourced from DrugBank, integrating predicted bioactivities with molecular efficiency metrics and pharmacokinetic profiling. To systematically prioritize the most viable lead candidates, the evaluation was structured across four quantitative filtering stages. Initially, strict binding affinity criteria were enforced to identify potent target inhibitors while mitigating off-target liabilities. A binding affinity threshold of $-9.54\text{ kcal/mol}$ (equivalent to $K_i = 0.1\text{ }\mu\text{M}$) was applied to select high-affinity compounds, while a baseline cutoff of $-8.18\text{ kcal/mol}$ ($K_i = 1\text{ }\mu\text{M}$) was utilized to screen out molecules with potential adverse side effects \cite{feng2023}.

Beyond raw potency, candidate prioritization deliberately incorporated dual efficiency metrics to prevent "molecular obesity" during high-throughput screening follow-ups \cite{hopkins2004}. Candidates were required to exceed an established Ligand Efficiency (LE) threshold of $0.3\text{ kcal/mol}$ per heavy atom \cite{hopkins2014}. Additionally, the Binding Efficiency Index (BEI) was utilized to substitute heavy atom count with total molecular weight ($\text{MW}$ in $\text{kDa}$), effectively correcting \textit{in vitro} activity for physicochemical property load and better accounting for heteroatom contributions \cite{abad2005, hopkins2014}. The BEI was computed as:
\begin{equation}
	\text{BEI} = \frac{pIC_{50}}{\text{MW}_{\text{kDa}}}
\end{equation}
where a reference target of approximately $27.0$ represents an idealized $1\text{ nM}$ potency ($pIC_{50} = 9.00$) at $0.333\text{ kDa}$ \cite{abad2005}. Because composite indices combining logarithmic potency values ($pIC_{50}$) with linear physical parameters introduce inherent size-dependent mathematical artifacts \cite{shultz2013}, BEI and LE were utilized strictly as secondary multi-objective ranking filters rather than primary objective functions. 

To explicitly control for collinearity and prevent molecular weight from artificially inflating either potency predictions or efficiency metrics, a strict physicochemical ceiling ($\text{MW} \le 1000\text{ Da}$) was enforced prior to screening. Furthermore, to ensure the model did not extrapolate into unsupported chemical spaces, the FDA library was strictly filtered to include only compounds matching the elemental composition of the training set (H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, I). Finally, an \texttt{IsolationForest} algorithm (5\% contamination threshold) was applied to restrict the remaining library strictly to the applicability domain of the training set. This rigorous pre-filtering ensures that the prioritized candidates are not statistical artifacts of structural autocorrelation or exotic chemistries, but rather genuine reflections of the training space's bioactivity distribution. To further balance potency against lipophilicity and mitigate the risk of nonspecific binding, Lipophilic Ligand Efficiency (LLE) was integrated into the scoring function. Only compounds maintaining an $\text{LLE} > 5$ (derived from $\text{cLogP}$ and binding constants) were prioritized to ensure a favorable physicochemical space \cite{hopkins2014}.

The virtual screening protocol concluded with a comprehensive predictive pharmacokinetic assessment. Candidates were first filtered through Lipinski's Rule of Five to ensure baseline oral bioavailability. Subsequently, machine learning models trained on Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) datasets were deployed to predict cardiac toxicity and intestinal permeability. Only compounds demonstrating a low probability of hERG channel blockade ($P < 0.5$) and moderate-to-high Caco-2 permeability ($\log P_{app} > -5.15\text{ cm/s}$) were advanced. Because a drug candidate with satisfactory therapeutic efficacy must also meet appropriate pharmacokinetic criteria, this final ADMET profiling acted as a critical filter to significantly reduce the risk of late-stage failure in the drug design process \cite{feng2023}.

\subsection{Orthogonal Physics-Based Validation via Molecular Docking}

To complement ligand-based machine learning predictions, an orthogonal structure-based evaluation using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 was introduced \cite{rajesh2026, trott2009}. This physical validation translates abstract chemical representations into spatial coordinates, mitigating the risk of false positives from scaffold memorization by ensuring genuine structural complementarity \cite{yang2019}. Vina employs an Iterated Local Search optimizer and a hybrid scoring function, calibrated against PDBbind data, to guide flexible ligands into optimal thermodynamic conformations \cite{eberhardt2021}.

Three-dimensional receptor coordinates were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (\textit{Toxoplasma gondii} DHFR, PDB ID: 6AOG, chain B) \cite{consortium2019, berman2003} and prepared using standard computational protocols \cite{berman2002}. The NADPH cofactor was retained within the catalytic pocket to accurately simulate the native biological environment. Following the removal of crystallographic water and local minimization, polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were assigned to generate the required PDBQT files \cite{damilola2025}. The Vina search space was centered on the native pyrimethamine (CP6) ligand coordinates ($x = 3.689, y = 39.992, z = -62.818$) with grid box dimensions set to $20 \times 20 \times 20\text{ \AA}$ and an exhaustiveness parameter of 32. This configuration allowed unrestricted exploration of ligand orientations and flexible rotatable bonds, thereby preventing crystal-pose centering bias \cite{trott2009}.

Prior to the virtual screening phase, the docking protocol was rigorously validated by re-docking the co-crystallized pyrimethamine ligand (CP6) back into the TgDHFR active site. The protocol successfully reproduced the native crystallographic binding mode, yielding a top-ranked pose with a centroid distance of $0.30\text{ \AA}$, well below the universally accepted $2.0\text{ \AA}$ RMSD threshold for structural fidelity.

Following validation, Vina evaluated and ranked the docked poses of the FDA candidates based on the predicted Gibbs free energy of binding ($\Delta G$) in kcal/mol, where lower energies denote stronger thermodynamic stability \cite{feng2023}. To systematically evaluate the consensus between the ligand-based and structure-based approaches, strict quantitative thresholds were established: compounds were classified as validated consensus hits only if they exhibited both a high ML-predicted potency ($pIC_{50} > 6.5$) and a strong docking affinity ($\Delta G < -8.0\text{ kcal/mol}$). Compounds meeting the ML threshold but failing the docking criteria were flagged as potential ML false positives. Furthermore, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the continuous $pIC_{50}$ predictions and the Vina docking scores to statistically quantify the global agreement between the two orthogonal methods. Finally, the physics-based energy was normalized against the heavy atom count to calculate Ligand Efficiency (LE), prioritizing compounds with $\text{LE} > 0.3\text{ kcal/mol}$ per heavy atom to ensure the selected inhibitors reside within a favorable, non-obese drug-like chemical space \cite{shultz2013, abad2005}.

\subsection{Computational Environment and Reproducibility}

To ensure full algorithmic transparency and computational reproducibility, all data curation, model training, and evaluation pipelines were programmatically implemented in Python (version 3.14.7). The computational environment was standardized using the following core software stack:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Cheminformatics and Featurization:} Molecular parsing, standardization, and descriptor generation (2D/3D/ECFP4) were executed using RDKit (version 2025.9.3).
	\item \textbf{Classical Machine Learning:} The nested cross-validation framework, hyperparameter Bayesian optimization, and baseline algorithms were built utilizing \texttt{scikit-learn} (version 1.8.0), \texttt{xgboost} (version 3.1.2), \texttt{lightgbm} (version 4.6.0), and \texttt{optuna}. Data handling was managed via \texttt{pandas} (version 2.3.3) and \texttt{numpy} (version 2.3.5).
	\item \textbf{Deep Learning Infrastructure:} The D-MPNN graph models were trained using \texttt{chemprop} (version 2.0) and \texttt{lightning}, with tensor operations supported by PyTorch (\texttt{torch} $\geq$ 2.0.0). 
	\item \textbf{Hardware Specifications:} Model training and evaluation were performed on a Ubuntu 26.04 LTS workstation equipped with Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2695 and utilizing CPU-only acceleration.
\end{itemize}
