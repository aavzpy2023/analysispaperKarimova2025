\subsection{Bioactivity Benchmark Dataset and Curation}

The dataset used in this study was derived from a previously published benchmark on \textit{Toxoplasma gondii} dihydrofolate reductase (TgDHFR) enzyme inhibitors \cite{karimova2025}. The provenance of these data relies on the integration of primary public repositories to ensure chemical and bioactivity representativeness, aligning with foundational QSAR guidelines that mandate rigorous data preparation \cite{tropsha2010}. By utilizing the exact baseline dataset compiled by Karimova and Senanayake \cite{karimova2025}, we establish a strict control framework. For the primary comparative benchmark, this methodological choice allows us to isolate and evaluate the true predictive power of classical machine learning algorithms against computationally heavy statistical techniques, deliberately omitting synthetic data augmentation to assess performance strictly on empirical ground-truth data.

The initial data collection was constructed by combining records from BindingDB, which provided detailed drug-protein interaction parameters and SMILES structures, alongside bioactivity data retrieved from ChEMBL \cite{karimova2025}. Following database integration, automated standardization steps---such as salt stripping and structural validation---were applied to guarantee overall chemical integrity \cite{damilola2025}. Crucially, of the initial 873 entries retrieved, 117 compounds were systematically excluded during this phase to mitigate experimental noise and avoid overfitting \cite{cherkasov2014, iwuala2025}. This specific exclusion was driven by two structural criteria: the presence of organometallic complexes that are fundamentally incompatible with subsequent 3D force-field generation, and the existence of undefined stereocenters that introduce unacceptable conformational ambiguity into the modeling pipeline.

To address the remaining compounds with multiple $IC_{50}$ measurements obtained under varying experimental conditions, the Interquartile Range (IQR) method was employed. Specifically, duplicate assays exhibiting a bioactivity variance exceeding 1.5 log units were filtered out as statistical outliers \cite{karimova2025}. For the remaining consistent entries, an average $IC_{50}$ value was calculated to establish a single, reliable representative measurement per ligand group. 

This rigorous curation protocol yielded a final, highly curated benchmark of $N = 756$ unique small molecules. The continuous target variable was formulated as $pIC_{50}$, defined as the negative logarithm of the molar $IC_{50}$ concentration ($pIC_{50} = -\log_{10}(IC_{50})$) \cite{karimova2025}. This logarithmic conversion standardizes the response values, reduces variance, and compresses the active range, rendering the target variable optimal for robust regression algorithms.

Finally, to explicitly quantify the performance impact of synthetic data generation reported in the original study, an isolated data augmentation protocol was implemented exclusively for post-benchmark ablation studies. Following a strict 15\% hold-out partition to prevent data leakage, the training features (excluding the target variable) were perturbed using additive Gaussian noise. Applying two distinct noise levels (standard deviations of $\sigma = 0.01$ and $\sigma = 0.001$) generated two synthetic variants per original molecule, effectively triplicating the training space while maintaining strict isolation of the empirical test set.

\subsection{Representation Spaces: Molecular Fingerprints and Conformational Descriptors}

To systematically evaluate the structure-activity relationship, four distinct molecular representation spaces were programmatically generated using the open-source cheminformatics library RDKit \cite{landrum2025}. The baseline purely structural representation utilized Morgan fingerprints (ECFP4), computed with a radius of 2 and folded into a fixed-length vector of \MorganNFeaturesTotal\ bits. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) were specifically developed for structure-activity modeling because they can be calculated rapidly and represent an essentially infinite number of distinct molecular features, including stereochemical information \cite{rogers2010}. Furthermore, classical 2D fingerprint-based models have been demonstrated to perform as well as state-of-the-art 3D structure-based models for bioactivity predictions relying solely on ligand information \cite{gao2020}.

To establish a quantitative physicochemical baseline, a comprehensive set of \RdkitTwoDNFeaturesTotal\ conventional two-dimensional topological representations was extracted. Well-curated 2D molecular representations are highly robust, and recent studies have shown they can maintain scaffold-robust advantages over 3D alternatives in various permeability and property prediction tasks \cite{tiwari2026}. To evaluate the synergistic effect of combining structural and physicochemical data, a third representation space was constructed by concatenating the \RdkitTwoDNFeaturesTotal\ 2D descriptors with the \MorganNFeaturesTotal-bit Morgan fingerprints.

Finally, to assess the predictive value of spatial geometry, a fourth hybrid representation was generated incorporating 3D conformational descriptors. Three-dimensional conformers were generated employing the Experimental-Torsion Knowledge Distance Geometry (ETKDGv3) approach implemented in RDKit \cite{riniker2015}. Because purely distance-based constraints tend to lead to distorted aromatic rings and $sp^2$ centers, this methodology utilizes experimental torsion-angle preferences obtained from small-molecule crystallographic data to improve conformation generation \cite{riniker2015}. Following embedding, the conformers were subjected to rigorous energy minimization using the MMFF94s force field to ensure thermodynamic stability prior to the extraction of 3D spatial descriptors. Although recent evaluations indicate that 3D conformer descriptors may provide limited incremental predictive value over curated 2D representations in specific tabular benchmark algorithms \cite{tiwari2026}, integrating spatial information remains highly relevant for molecular image-based modeling \cite{matsu2023} and geometry-augmented representation learning \cite{yanan2024}.

\subsection{15 Fold Nested Cross-Validation Protocol}

In a standard cross-validation protocol, the dataset is partitioned into $k$ disjoint blocks that are systematically rotated between training and testing phases \cite{calle2025, krstajic2014}. While conventional nested cross-validation (NCV) literature frequently employs symmetrical $5 \times 5$ or $10 \times 10$ architectures \cite{calle2025, lewis2023, wainer2021}, there is no methodological restriction requiring identical fold counts for the inner and outer loops \cite{krstajic2014}. To guarantee leak-free evaluation, balance computational cost, and mitigate model selection bias in this low-data regime ($N = \NCompounds$), a strict $\TotalOuterFolds \times \InnerKFolds$ asymmetric NCV protocol was implemented (\OuterKFolds\ outer folds repeated \OuterRepeats\ times). This architecture mathematically decouples model assessment from model selection using two distinct concentric loops.

The choice of data partitioning strategy is critical in small-scale QSAR modeling \cite{kyrali2022}. While Bemis-Murcko scaffold splitting is widely advocated to test out-of-domain extrapolation and prevent structural leakage between folds \cite{yang2019, aga2025, beatrice2026}, a random nested cross-validation scheme was deliberately selected for this study. In a highly restricted dataset of \NCompounds\ compounds, scaffold splitting frequently generates severely imbalanced folds, creating artificial ``activity cliffs'' where entire bioactivity ranges are absent from the training pool. This instability prevents the inner optimization loop from converging on generalizable hyperparameters. Because the primary objective of this model is interpolation within the established applicability domain of known TgDHFR inhibitors---rather than extrapolation to entirely novel chemotypes---random splitting ensures that the bioactivity distribution remains statistically consistent across all folds, enabling robust and stable hyperparameter tuning.

The outer loop of the NCV framework was designed for generalization assessment. To achieve a lower variance in performance estimation compared to extreme approaches like Leave-One-Out (LOO) \cite{krstajic2014}, the dataset was partitioned into \TotalOuterFolds\ independent outer splits. In each iteration, one fold is strictly held out as the test set while the remaining training pool is passed into the inner loop. This strict isolation ensures that the test data is never exposed during feature selection or model selection, yielding a truly unbiased out-of-sample prediction estimate \cite{varma2006, wainer2021}.

The inner loop was dedicated exclusively to architecture selection and model optimization. Within each outer training pool, a secondary \InnerKFolds-fold inner loop was embedded to evaluate all single, pairwise, and three-way stacking combinations (up to \MaxComboSize\ base estimators) drawn from a pool of six diverse regressor families: Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees (ET), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Regression (SVR with RBF kernel, $C=10$), and $k$-Nearest Neighbors (kNN with cosine distance). For multi-model combinations, meta-learning was coordinated via a \texttt{StackingRegressor} utilizing \texttt{RidgeCV} as the meta-estimator. Confining model selection, descriptor scaling (\texttt{StandardScaler}), median imputation, and positive-importance feature selection entirely within the inner partitions prevents inadvertent information leakage from the test set, guaranteeing that reported metrics reflect genuine generalizability rather than selection artifacts \cite{parvandeh2020, lewis2023, calle2025}.

\subsection{Deep Learning Baseline: Directed Message Passing Neural Networks}

To benchmark the classical machine learning ensembles against advanced representation learning, we implemented a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) using the Chemprop framework (version 2.0) \cite{graff2026, yang2019}. To guarantee absolute methodological parity and eliminate evaluation bias, the D-MPNN was subjected to the exact same 15-fold cross-validation protocol utilized for the classical baselines. While executing such rigorous resampling on deep learning architectures incurs substantial computational overhead, this strict alignment ensures that the resulting performance metrics are directly comparable and entirely free from single-split variance artifacts.

The architecture processes molecular SMILES strings by mapping them into graph representations via RDKit, designating atoms as vertices and bonds as edges \cite{heid2024}. Initial feature vectors, capturing atomic identity and topological connectivity, were constructed using the \texttt{SimpleMoleculeMolGraphFeaturizer()}. The network subsequently applied \texttt{BondMessagePassing()} to extract embeddings directly from directed edges rather than vertices. This edge-centric design eliminates spurious feedback loops (totters), thereby reducing noise in the learned molecular representations \cite{yang2019}. Furthermore, a \texttt{MeanAggregation()} function pooled these atomic embeddings into a unified molecular vector.

Prior to training, the continuous $pIC_{50}$ targets required internal normalization. The aggregated embeddings fed into a \texttt{RegressionFFN()} equipped with batch normalization to yield the final predictive output. Model weights were optimized via PyTorch Lightning over a maximum of 100 epochs. We deliberately retained this epoch limit and the default Chemprop hyperparameters (e.g., hidden size, depth, and learning rate) to establish a solid, uninflated baseline. Extensive hyperparameter tuning for deep neural networks on datasets of this scale frequently induces severe memorization rather than genuine generalization \cite{cawley2010, hadas2023}. Thus, evaluating the network under standard configurations provides a highly conservative benchmark against the classical ensembles.

The internal loss function optimized during training was Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Out-of-sample performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) averaged across all \TotalOuterFolds\ folds. This rigorous statistical protocol yielded a final cross-validated test performance of $R^2 = \ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoMean \pm \ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoStd$ and an MAE of \ChemPropDMPNNFoldMae, establishing a highly robust, variance-aware deep learning baseline for subsequent comparative analysis.

\subsection{Virtual Screening Protocol and Ligand Efficiency Profiling}

To systematically prioritize viable lead candidates from an FDA-approved compound library, we deployed an automated virtual screening pipeline driven by the highest-performing classical machine learning ensemble (\WinnerMode, nested CV $R^2 = \WinnerRTwoMean$). Rather than relying solely on raw predicted bioactivity, the evaluation framework integrated strict applicability domain enforcement with multi-objective ligand efficiency profiling and predictive pharmacokinetics. This approach mitigates the risk of prioritizing ``molecularly obese" compounds that exhibit high theoretical potency but possess unfavorable physicochemical properties for downstream development \cite{hopkins2004}.

The screening protocol initiated with a rigorous structural pre-filtering phase. To prevent the model from extrapolating into unsupported chemical spaces, the FDA library was restricted to compounds matching the exact elemental composition of the training set (H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, I). Additionally, a strict physicochemical ceiling (molecular weight $\le 1000$ Da) was enforced to control for collinearity and prevent molecular size from artificially inflating potency predictions. 

In this sense, to ensure the statistical validity of the predictions, we defined the model's applicability domain using an unsupervised anomaly detection approach. An \texttt{IsolationForest} algorithm, configured with a 5\% contamination threshold, was trained on the Morgan fingerprint matrix of the original training set. This algorithm filtered the FDA library to exclude structural outliers, ensuring that all advanced candidates resided strictly within the interpolated chemical space of the known bioactivity distribution. The predictive accuracy of the ensemble within this domain was internally validated by recovering and accurately predicting the $pIC_{50}$ values of known reference inhibitors, such as Pyrimethamine and Trimethoprim, prior to ranking the unknown candidates.

Following $pIC_{50}$ prediction, candidate prioritization deliberately incorporated four distinct efficiency metrics to correct \textit{in vitro} activity for physicochemical load. Ligand Efficiency (LE) quantified the binding energy per heavy atom count (HAC), requiring candidates to demonstrate optimal atomic utilization \cite{hopkins2014}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{LE} = \frac{1.37 \times pIC_{50}}{\text{HAC}}
\end{equation}
To account for heteroatom contributions and total mass, the Binding Efficiency Index (BEI) substituted HAC with molecular weight (MW) \cite{abad2005}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{BEI} = \frac{pIC_{50}}{\text{MW}} \times 1000
\end{equation}
Furthermore, to balance potency against lipophilicity and mitigate the risk of nonspecific binding, Lipophilic Ligand Efficiency (LLE) was computed as the difference between predicted potency and the calculated partition coefficient ($\text{LLE} = pIC_{50} - \text{cLogP}$). Finally, the Surface Efficiency Index (SEI) evaluated potency relative to the topological polar surface area (TPSA), providing a proxy for membrane permeability potential ($\text{SEI} = (pIC_{50} / \text{TPSA}) \times 100$). Because composite indices combining logarithmic potency values with linear physical parameters can introduce size-dependent mathematical artifacts \cite{shultz2013}, these four metrics (LE, BEI, LLE, SEI) were utilized strictly as secondary multi-objective ranking filters rather than primary objective functions. 

The virtual screening protocol concluded with a comprehensive predictive pharmacokinetic (ADMET) assessment to reduce late-stage attrition risks. Candidates were first evaluated against Lipinski's Rule of Five criteria, strictly filtering out molecules exceeding 5 hydrogen bond donors, 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, or a calculated LogP of 5. To assess critical toxicity and absorption endpoints, custom machine learning models were trained using curated datasets from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC). Molecular structures were encoded as 2048-bit Morgan fingerprints (radius 2) and processed through Random Forest architectures comprising 100 estimators. A \texttt{RandomForestClassifier} trained on the TDC hERG dataset evaluated cardiac toxicity risk, advancing only compounds with a low probability of hERG channel blockade ($P < 0.5$). Concurrently, a \texttt{RandomForestRegressor} trained on the Caco2-Wang dataset predicted intestinal absorption, requiring candidates to demonstrate moderate-to-high permeability ($\log P_{app} > -5.15\text{ cm/s}$). This final ADMET profiling ensured that the prioritized FDA candidates represent not only highly efficient binders but also structurally validated, orally bioavailable starting points for subsequent molecular docking.

\subsection{Orthogonal Physics-Based Validation via Molecular Docking}

To complement the ligand-based machine learning predictions, we implemented an orthogonal structure-based evaluation using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 \cite{rajesh2026, trott2009}. This physical validation translates abstract chemical representations into spatial coordinates, mitigating the risk of false positives induced by algorithmic scaffold memorization. By ensuring genuine structural complementarity, the docking protocol acts as a thermodynamic filter. Vina employs an Iterated Local Search optimizer and a hybrid scoring function, calibrated against PDBbind data, to guide flexible ligands into optimal binding conformations \cite{eberhardt2021}.

Three-dimensional receptor coordinates were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (\textit{Toxoplasma gondii} DHFR, PDB ID: 6AOG, chain \RedockChain) \cite{consortium2019, berman2003}. To accurately simulate the native biological environment, the NADPH cofactor was retained within the catalytic pocket. Following the removal of crystallographic water and local minimization, polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were assigned to generate the required PDBQT files \cite{damilola2025}. The Vina search space was centered on the native \RedockLigand ligand coordinates ($x = \DockCenterX, y = \DockCenterY, z = \DockCenterZ$) with grid box dimensions set to $\DockBoxSize \times \DockBoxSize \times \DockBoxSize\text{ \AA}$ and an exhaustiveness parameter of \DockExhaustiveness. This configuration permitted unrestricted exploration of ligand orientations and flexible rotatable bonds, thereby preventing crystal-pose centering bias \cite{trott2009}.

Prior to screening the machine learning candidates, the docking protocol underwent rigorous validation via re-docking of the co-crystallized \RedockLigand ligand into the TgDHFR active site. The protocol successfully reproduced the native crystallographic binding mode, yielding a top-ranked pose with a centroid distance of $\RedockRMSD\text{ \AA}$. This deviation falls well below the universally accepted $\RedockThreshold\text{ \AA}$ RMSD threshold, confirming the structural fidelity of the grid parameters and the reliability of the scoring function for this specific target.

Following validation, Vina evaluated the 3D conformers of the \DockNTotal\ prioritized FDA candidates, ranking poses based on the predicted Gibbs free energy of binding ($\Delta G$) in kcal/mol, where lower energies denote stronger thermodynamic stability \cite{feng2023}. To systematically evaluate the consensus between the ligand-based and structure-based approaches, we established strict quantitative thresholds. Compounds were classified as validated consensus hits only if they exhibited both high ML-predicted potency ($pIC_{50} > 6.5$) and strong docking affinity ($\Delta G < -8.0\text{ kcal/mol}$). Compounds meeting the ML threshold but failing the docking criteria were flagged as potential ML false positives. Finally, to statistically quantify the monotonic agreement between the two orthogonal methods without assuming linearity, a Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted between the continuous $pIC_{50}$ predictions and the Vina docking scores ($\rho = \DockSpearmanRho$, $p = \DockSpearmanP$).

\subsection{Computational Environment and Reproducibility}

To ensure full algorithmic transparency and computational reproducibility \cite{rojas2026}, all data curation, model training, and evaluation pipelines were programmatically implemented in Python (version 3.14.7). The computational environment was standardized using the following core software stack:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Cheminformatics and Featurization:} Molecular parsing, standardization, and descriptor generation (2D/3D/ECFP4) were executed using RDKit (version 2025.9.3) \cite{feng2023}.
	\item \textbf{Classical Machine Learning:} The nested cross-validation framework \cite{krstajic2014, calle2025}, hyperparameter Bayesian optimization \cite{bauman2014}, and baseline algorithms were built utilizing \texttt{scikit-learn} (version 1.8.0) \cite{brva2026}, \texttt{xgboost} (version 3.1.2) \cite{wainer2021}, \texttt{lightgbm} (version 4.6.0) \cite{rajesh2026}, and \texttt{optuna} \cite{tyler2025}. Data handling was managed via \texttt{pandas} (version 2.3.3) and \texttt{numpy} (version 2.3.5) \cite{eberhardt2021}.
	\item \textbf{Deep Learning Infrastructure:} The D-MPNN graph models were trained using \texttt{chemprop} (version 2.0) \cite{graff2026, heid2024} and \texttt{lightning}, with tensor operations supported by PyTorch (\texttt{torch} ($\geq$) 2.0.0) \cite{thomas2024}. 
	\item \textbf{Hardware Specifications:} Model training and evaluation were performed on a Ubuntu 26.04 LTS workstation equipped with Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2695 and utilizing CPU-only acceleration.
\end{itemize}
