\subsection{Bioactivity Benchmark Dataset and Curation}
\label{sec:materials_curation}

This study utilizes a previously published benchmark of \textit{Toxoplasma gondii} dihydrofolate reductase (TgDHFR) enzyme inhibitors \cite{karimova2025}. Adopting the baseline dataset compiled by Karimova and Senanayake establishes a rigorous methodological control. This framework isolates the predictive capacity of classical machine learning algorithms from computationally heavy statistical interventions. Omitting synthetic data augmentation during the primary benchmarking phase ensures that performance metrics reflect empirical ground-truth data rather than algorithmic artifacts.

Initial records merged drug-protein interaction parameters and SMILES structures from BindingDB with bioactivity data from ChEMBL \cite{karimova2025}. Automated standardization protocols, including salt stripping and structural validation, ensured chemical integrity \cite{damilola2025}. The merged table contained \DataNRaw\ curated records. Extreme potency values were screened with Tukey's rule on their $pIC_{50}$ distribution (fences at Q1 $-$ 1.5\,IQR $= \OutlierFenceLow$ and Q3 $+$ 1.5\,IQR $= \OutlierFenceHigh$). \DataNOutliers\ records fell outside the fences: a classical antifolate analogue at $pIC_{50} = \OutlierHighPIC$ (high fence) and a practically inactive compound at $pIC_{50} = \OutlierLowPIC$ (low fence). They were excluded from the primary analysis, which leaves \DataNAfterOutliers\ entries, the size reported in the original study \cite{karimova2025}. Because an exclusion of this kind can itself shape the reported performance, the full benchmark was also run with these two records retained (Section~\ref{sec:outlier_sensitivity}).

% TODO(author): confirm that the repeated-measurement IQR step below describes how the curated table was built (it cannot be re-run from this repository, which starts from the curated table).

For compounds exhibiting multiple $IC_{50}$ measurements across varying experimental conditions, we applied the Interquartile Range (IQR) method. Duplicate assays with a bioactivity variance exceeding 1.5 log units were discarded as statistical outliers \cite{karimova2025}. The remaining consistent entries were averaged to yield a single representative measurement per ligand. 

To adhere to the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) principles for QSAR validation \cite{tropsha2010, cherkasov2014, iwuala2025} and to keep the same structure out of both training and test folds, all SMILES were then canonicalized with RDKit. The \DataNAfterOutliers\ entries turned out to describe only \DataNMain\ distinct structures: \DataNDuplicatesRemoved\ molecules appeared twice under different PubChem CIDs, and the second occurrence of each was removed (the first was retained). The measured $pIC_{50}$ values within each pair differed by at most \DataDuplicateMaxSpread\ units, so the target distribution is practically unchanged. The removed records are listed in \texttt{data/V2-combined\_bd\_chmbl\_unique\_duplicates.csv}.

This curation protocol yielded a final benchmark of $\NCompounds$ unique small molecules. The continuous target variable was transformed into $pIC_{50}$ ($-\log_{10}(IC_{50})$) \cite{karimova2025}. This logarithmic conversion standardizes response values and compresses the active range, optimizing the target distribution for robust regression modeling.

To explicitly quantify the performance impact of the interventions reported in the original literature, we replicated its hold-out protocol in an isolated ablation study. A 15\% hold-out partition was separated first (seed 42), and everything below was learned from the development partition only. The baseline (A) is the stacking ensemble on all \MorganNFeaturesTotal\ Morgan bits. The feature-selection variant (B) computes the permutation importance of every bit on the development partition (5 repeats, negative mean squared error) and keeps the bits whose mean importance is positive beyond floating-point noise (greater than $10^{-9}$), because bits that the model never uses have an importance that is zero up to rounding error. The augmentation variant (C) adds, to the selected features, perturbation of the training features via additive Gaussian noise: applying two distinct noise levels ($\sigma = 0.01$ and $\sigma = 0.001$) generated two synthetic variants per original molecule, which effectively triplicated the training space (to \ExpCAugSizeTrain\ rows) while maintaining absolute isolation of the empirical test set. Each variant is the average of five stacking ensembles that differ in the seed of their tree models.

\subsection{Representation Spaces: Molecular Fingerprints and Conformational Descriptors}

We generated four distinct molecular representation spaces using RDKit \cite{landrum2025} to systematically map the structure-activity relationship. The structural baseline relied on Morgan fingerprints (ECFP4). We computed these with a radius of 2 and folded them into a fixed-length vector of \MorganNFeaturesTotal\ bits. Extended-connectivity fingerprints offer rapid calculation and capture a vast array of distinct local substructural features \cite{rogers2010}; chirality was not encoded. Classical 2D fingerprint-based models frequently achieve predictive parity with state-of-the-art 3D structure-based models when relying exclusively on ligand information \cite{gao2020}.

To establish a physicochemical baseline, the pipeline extracted the complete RDKit descriptor list (\RdkitTwoDNFeaturesTotal\ conventional two-dimensional topological and physicochemical descriptors). Curated 2D molecular representations demonstrate substantial robustness; recent studies confirm they maintain scaffold-independent advantages over 3D alternatives across various property prediction tasks \cite{tiwari2026}. A third hybrid space concatenated these \RdkitTwoDNFeaturesTotal\ descriptors with the \MorganNFeaturesTotal-bit Morgan fingerprints to evaluate potential synergistic effects.

A fourth representation appended ten 3D shape descriptors (asphericity, eccentricity, inertial shape factor, the two normalized principal moment ratios, the three principal moments of inertia, radius of gyration, and spherocity index) to this hybrid, for \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpNFeaturesTotal\ features in total, to assess the predictive value of spatial geometry. Hydrogens were added and a single conformer per molecule was generated with the Experimental-Torsion Knowledge Distance Geometry algorithm (ETKDGv3, fixed random seed of 42, random initial coordinates as a fallback) \cite{riniker2015}. Because purely distance-based constraints often distort aromatic rings and $sp^2$ centers, ETKDGv3 integrates experimental torsion-angle preferences derived from small-molecule crystallographic data. The conformer was then locally minimized with the MMFF94 force field before descriptor extraction. Conformer embedding failed for \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpNThreeDFailed\ of the \DataNMain\ molecules, and \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpNCellsMissing\ descriptor values of the 2D+3D+FP matrix were missing or infinite before imputation.

Although integrating spatial information remains critical for molecular image-based modeling \cite{matsu2023} and geometry-augmented representation learning \cite{yanan2024}, recent empirical evaluations suggest that 3D conformer descriptors yield limited incremental predictive value over curated 2D representations in standard tabular benchmarks \cite{tiwari2026}. Our nested cross-validation framework explicitly tested this hypothesis. The purely structural Morgan fingerprints yielded a mean $R^2$ of \MorganRTwoMean, without a significant difference from the 3D hybrid space ($R^2 = \RdkitTwoDThreeDFpRTwoMean$; Nadeau-Bengio-corrected paired test on the per-fold MAE, $t = \CorrectedTStat$, $p = \CorrectedTP$). The two best-scoring spaces, the 2D descriptors with fingerprints and the 3D hybrid, were likewise indistinguishable from each other ($p = \PairedTTestP$). Since no space was reliably superior and the Morgan space needs neither descriptor calculation nor conformer generation, it was adopted for all downstream feature selection, data augmentation, and virtual screening protocols.

\subsection{Nested Cross-Validation Protocol and Variance Correction}
\label{sec:materials_ncv}

Evaluating machine learning architectures in low-data regimes demands rigorous isolation between model optimization and performance estimation. Standard cross-validation partitions datasets into $k$ disjoint blocks, systematically rotating them between training and testing phases \cite{calle2025, krstajic2014}. While conventional nested cross-validation (NCV) literature frequently defaults to symmetrical $5 \times 5$ or $10 \times 10$ architectures \cite{calle2025, lewis2023, wainer2021}, methodological frameworks do not mandate identical fold counts for inner and outer loops \cite{krstajic2014}. To guarantee leak-free evaluation, balance computational overhead, and mitigate the model selection bias described in Section 1.3, we implemented a strict $\OuterRepeats \times \OuterKFolds$ repeated nested cross-validation protocol for this restricted dataset ($N = \NCompounds$). This architecture executes a \OuterKFolds-fold outer loop repeated \OuterRepeats\ times (random seed 42), embedding a \InnerKFolds-fold inner loop, mathematically decoupling model assessment from model selection. The same outer partitions were used for every molecular representation, which allows paired comparisons between them.

Data partitioning strategies dictate the validity of small-scale QSAR models \cite{kyrali2022}. Bemis-Murcko scaffold splitting is widely advocated to test out-of-domain extrapolation and prevent structural leakage \cite{yang2019, aga2025, beatrice2026}. However, given the restricted size of this benchmark ($N = \NCompounds$), scaffold-based partitioning is expected to generate imbalanced folds, in which entire bioactivity ranges could vanish from the training pool. Our primary objective is interpolation within the established applicability domain of known TgDHFR inhibitors, rather than extrapolation to entirely novel chemotypes, so random splitting was adopted for the main benchmark, which keeps the bioactivity distribution consistent across folds. Because random splits are known to be optimistic for series of close analogs, we also repeated the Morgan-fingerprint ensemble and the D-MPNN under scaffold-grouped splits. The Bemis-Murcko scaffold of each compound (without stereochemistry) defined a group; the \NCompounds\ compounds fall into \ScaffoldNGroups\ groups, the largest with \ScaffoldLargestGroup\ members, and \ScaffoldSingletonGroups\ groups contain a single compound. In each of the \OuterRepeats\ repeats, groups were visited in random order and each was assigned to the currently smallest of the \OuterKFolds\ folds, so that no scaffold appears in both the training and the test partition of an outer fold. Everything else (the \TotalOuterFolds\ outer folds, the inner loop with random folds, the hyperparameters and the corrected tests) was identical to the random-split protocol, and both model families again shared the same folds.

The outer loop of the NCV framework evaluates generalization. To achieve lower variance in performance estimation compared to extreme approaches like Leave-One-Out (LOO) \cite{krstajic2014}, we evaluated \TotalOuterFolds\ outer splits. Standard parametric tests assume independent samples. Repeated cross-validation violates this assumption via overlapping training sets across iterations. To neutralize this intra-repetition correlation, we applied the Nadeau and Bengio variance correction \cite{nadeau1999} across all downstream statistical comparisons: the variance of the mean difference over the $J = \TotalOuterFolds$ outer folds is inflated from $\hat{\sigma}^2/J$ to $(1/J + n_{test}/n_{train})\,\hat{\sigma}^2$, with $n_{test}/n_{train} = 1/4$ for the 5-fold outer loop. In each iteration, one fold is strictly held out as the test set while the remaining pool passes into the inner loop. This absolute isolation ensures the test data remains hidden during feature and model selection, yielding a nearly unbiased out-of-sample prediction estimate \cite{varma2006, wainer2021}.

The inner loop governed architecture selection. Within each outer training pool, an embedded \InnerKFolds-fold inner loop evaluated all \NArchitectureCombos\ single, pairwise, and three-way stacking combinations (up to \MaxComboSize\ base estimators) and retained the combination with the highest mean inner-fold $R^2$. These combinations were drawn from six diverse regressor families: Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees (ET), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Regression (SVR with RBF kernel, $C=10$), and $k$-Nearest Neighbors (kNN, $k=5$, cosine distance). The tree ensembles used 200 trees; all hyperparameters were fixed in advance and none was tuned, so that the inner loop selects among architectures only. For multi-model combinations, a \texttt{StackingRegressor} utilizing \texttt{RidgeCV} as the meta-estimator, trained on 5-fold out-of-fold base predictions, coordinated the meta-learning phase. The selected combination was then refit on the whole outer training pool and scored once on the held-out outer fold. Predictions were clipped to the training range extended by 3 $pIC_{50}$ units as a safeguard; this affected \NClippedPredictions\ predictions across all outer folds.

This inner loop also serves as a structural safeguard against preprocessing leakage. Executing global transformations---such as z-score standardization, median imputation, or supervised feature selection---prior to fold partitioning inadvertently leaks dataset-wide statistics into the training phase \cite{cawley2010, varma2006}. To eliminate this vulnerability, every transformation was implemented as a step of the model pipeline, so that its parameters were computed only on the training partition at hand (an inner training fold during selection, the outer training pool for the final fit) and subsequently applied to the validation or test data without re-estimation \cite{brva2026}. For the continuous descriptor spaces, the pipeline chained median imputation, standardization (\texttt{StandardScaler}), and feature selection that kept every descriptor with a strictly positive importance in a Random Forest (\texttt{SelectFromModel}) before the regressor; the binary Morgan space was passed to the regressors unchanged. By confining model selection and these transformations entirely within the training partitions, we prevent inadvertent information leakage. This strict encapsulation guarantees that reported metrics reflect genuine generalizability rather than selection artifacts \cite{parvandeh2020, lewis2023, calle2025}.

\subsection{Deep learning baseline: Directed message passing neural networks}

Benchmarking classical machine learning ensembles against advanced representation learning required implementing a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) via the Chemprop framework (version 2.2.4) \cite{graff2026, yang2019}. To guarantee methodological parity, the D-MPNN was evaluated on the same \TotalOuterFolds\ outer partitions (\OuterRepeats$\times$\OuterKFolds\ repeated cross-validation, random seed 42) used for the classical baselines, with each molecule predicted by a model that never saw it. Two variants were run: a default configuration, for which no inner loop was needed, and a tuned variant with a hyperparameter search nested inside each outer fold (see below). This alignment keeps the performance metrics directly comparable and free from single-split variance artifacts.

The architecture processes molecular SMILES strings by mapping them into graph representations, designating atoms as vertices and bonds as edges \cite{heid2024}. Initial feature vectors capturing atomic identity and topological connectivity were constructed using \texttt{SimpleMoleculeMolGraphFeaturizer()}. The network applied \texttt{BondMessagePassing()} (hidden size 300, depth 3, no dropout) to extract embeddings directly from directed edges rather than vertices. This edge-centric design eliminates spurious feedback loops, reducing noise in the learned molecular representations \cite{yang2019}. A \texttt{MeanAggregation()} function pooled these atomic embeddings into a unified molecular vector.

Within each training partition, the continuous $pIC_{50}$ targets were standardized with the training mean and standard deviation, and predictions were transformed back to the original scale. The pooled vector was batch-normalized and fed into a \texttt{RegressionFFN()} (one hidden layer of 300 units) to yield the final predictive output. The mean squared error on the standardized targets was the training loss. Model weights were optimized via PyTorch Lightning for a fixed number of 100 epochs on CPU, using Chemprop's default learning-rate schedule (2 warm-up epochs, initial rate $10^{-4}$, peak $10^{-3}$, final $10^{-4}$) and a per-fold random seed (42 plus the fold index). No early stopping or checkpoint selection was applied, so the model after the last epoch was used and the held-out fold never influenced training.

Unlike the classical ensembles, whose architecture was selected by an inner search, the default D-MPNN used Chemprop's default hyperparameters (hidden size, depth and dropout as above). Quantitatively, one D-MPNN training took on average \GnnFoldSecMean\ seconds (range \GnnFoldSecMin--\GnnFoldSecMax\ s) on the workstation CPU, so the \TotalOuterFolds\ folds required about \GnnTotalMin\ minutes. A search matching the classical one, with \GnnSearchFits\ trainings per outer fold (\NArchitectureCombos\ architecture combinations across \InnerKFolds\ inner splits), would take roughly \GnnSearchDaysTotal\ days for the full protocol at that training time, which is why the tuned variant uses a reduced search.

The tuned variant searched a grid of \GnnTunedNConfigs\ configurations (hidden size 300 or 600, message-passing depth 3 or 5, dropout 0 or 0.2; the default configuration is one of them). Inside each outer training pool, 20\% of the compounds were held out at random, every configuration was trained on the remaining 80\% and the one with the lowest root-mean-square error on the held-out part was retained. That configuration was then retrained on the whole outer training pool, with the same 100-epoch schedule and per-fold seed, and scored once on the outer test fold. The held-out outer fold therefore played no role in the choice. The search is deliberately smaller than the classical one (a single inner split instead of \InnerKFolds\ folds, and \GnnTunedNConfigs\ configurations instead of \NArchitectureCombos\ combinations), so it tests whether a moderate amount of tuning helps rather than the best result that exhaustive tuning could reach. One tuned outer fold took on average \GnnTunedFoldSecMean\ s, and the complete tuned benchmark amounted to \GnnTunedTotalHours\ h of worker time (two training processes ran in parallel). For comparison, a classical outer fold including its complete inner search took between \ClassicalFoldSecMin\ and \ClassicalFoldSecMax\ seconds on average, depending on the representation (wall-clock times on the same machine, not normalized by thread count). The default variant isolates the intrinsic inductive bias of the graph architecture from the confounding artifacts of small-sample tuning \cite{cawley2010, hadas2023}, and the tuned variant checks whether that choice handicapped the network.

Out-of-sample performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) averaged across all \TotalOuterFolds\ folds. This protocol yielded a final cross-validated test performance of $R^2 = \ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoMean \pm \ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoStd$ and an MAE of \ChemPropDMPNNFoldMae, establishing the deep learning baseline for subsequent comparative analysis.

\subsection{Virtual Screening Protocol and Ligand Efficiency Profiling}

An automated virtual screening pipeline, driven by the feature-selected Morgan-fingerprint stacking ensemble of the augmentation study (Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVR combined by a ridge meta-learner, trained without augmentation on the \ExpNDev-compound development partition of the 15\% hold-out split; hold-out $R^2 = \ExpBRTwoMean$), systematically prioritized viable lead candidates from an FDA-approved library ($N = \ScreenNLibrary$). Rather than relying solely on raw predicted bioactivity, the evaluation framework integrated applicability domain enforcement with multi-objective ligand efficiency profiling. This approach mitigates the risk of prioritizing ``molecularly obese'' compounds that exhibit high theoretical potency but possess unfavorable physicochemical properties for downstream development \cite{hopkins2004}.

Initial structural pre-filtering restricted the FDA library to compounds containing only H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, and I, a set that covers all but \ScreenTrainNOutsideElements\ of the training compounds. This constraint prevents the model from extrapolating into unsupported chemical spaces. A size ceiling (molecular weight $\le \ScreenMaxMW$ Da; the largest training compound weighs \ScreenTrainMaxMW\ Da) removed very large molecules whose potency the model could not meaningfully extrapolate.

To enforce the applicability domain quantitatively, an \texttt{IsolationForest} algorithm (\ScreenIsoContamination\% contamination threshold, fixed seed) was fitted on the fingerprints of all \ScreenNTrainMols\ curated compounds, restricted to the same selected Morgan bits (\ExpBNFeaturesSelected\ of \ExpBNFeaturesTotal) used by the deployed ensemble, and evaluated the remaining FDA compounds. This unsupervised anomaly detection excluded compounds lying in sparsely populated regions of the training fingerprint space, so that all advanced candidates resided within the region of chemical space covered by the known bioactivity data. Ultimately, this hierarchical funnel reduced the initial library of \ScreenNLibrary\ compounds to \ScreenNStructural\ after the size and elemental filters, to \ScreenNDomain\ inside the applicability domain, and finally to \ScreenNCandidates\ candidates for orthogonal docking validation: the \ScreenNTopML\ top-ranked compounds by predicted potency (which already included methotrexate, pyrimethamine, and triamterene from the candidate list of the original study) plus \ScreenNReference\ further reference entries from that list (trimethoprim as base, sulfate, hydrochloride, and sulfamethoxazole combination, finerenone, etodolac, and bisacodyl). As a consistency check, the ensemble recovered the experimental $pIC_{50}$ of the reference inhibitors pyrimethamine (predicted \ControlPyrimethaminePred\ versus \ControlPyrimethamineExp) and trimethoprim (\ControlTrimethoprimPred\ versus \ControlTrimethoprimExp); both compounds belong to the model's training partition, so this verifies fit and not generalization.

Four distinct efficiency metrics were then computed to contextualize predicted activity for physicochemical load. Ligand Efficiency (LE) quantified the binding energy per heavy atom count (HAC) \cite{hopkins2014}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{LE} = \frac{1.37 \times pIC_{50}}{\text{HAC}}
\end{equation}
To account for heteroatom contributions and total mass, the Binding Efficiency Index (BEI) substituted HAC with molecular weight (MW) \cite{abad2005}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{BEI} = \frac{pIC_{50}}{\text{MW}} \times 1000
\end{equation}
Furthermore, to balance potency against lipophilicity and mitigate the risk of nonspecific binding, Lipophilic Ligand Efficiency (LLE) was computed as the difference between predicted potency and the calculated partition coefficient ($\text{LLE} = pIC_{50} - \text{cLogP}$, Crippen method in RDKit). Finally, the Surface Efficiency Index (SEI) evaluated potency relative to the topological polar surface area (TPSA), providing a proxy for membrane permeability potential ($\text{SEI} = (pIC_{50} / \text{TPSA}) \times 100$). Because composite indices combining logarithmic potency values with linear physical parameters can introduce size-dependent mathematical artifacts \cite{shultz2013}, these four metrics (LE, BEI, LLE, SEI) were computed from the predicted $pIC_{50}$ and reported alongside it for interpretation; candidates were ranked and selected by predicted potency, and none of the metrics was used as a filter.

\subsection{Orthogonal Physics-Based Validation via Molecular Docking}

Orthogonal structure-based evaluation via AutoDock Vina v1.2.7 \cite{rajesh2026, trott2009} complemented the ligand-based predictions. This physical validation translates abstract chemical representations into spatial coordinates, mitigating the risk of false positives induced by algorithmic scaffold memorization. By assessing structural complementarity with the binding site, the docking protocol acts as an orthogonal plausibility filter. Vina employs an Iterated Local Search optimizer and a hybrid scoring function, calibrated against PDBbind data, to guide flexible ligands into optimal binding conformations \cite{eberhardt2021}.

Three-dimensional receptor coordinates were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (\textit{Toxoplasma gondii} DHFR, PDB ID: 6AOG) \cite{consortium2019, berman2003}. The receptor was prepared as a rigid apo structure: all non-protein records (crystallographic waters, ions, cofactors including the bound NADPH, and the co-crystallized ligand) were removed, retaining both protein chains. The structure was converted to the PDBQT format with OpenBabel in rigid mode \cite{babel2011}, keeping heavy atoms only: no hydrogens were added and no partial charges were assigned, which the Vina scoring function does not use. While excluding the NADPH cofactor omits a potential source of active-site polarization, this apo-receptor approximation is a common simplification in structure-based virtual screening. The Vina search space was centered on the coordinates of the native \RedockLigand{} ligand in chain \RedockChain{} ($x = \DockCenterX{}, y = \DockCenterY{}, z = \DockCenterZ$) with a cubic grid box of $\DockBoxSize{} \times \DockBoxSize{} \times \DockBoxSize{}\text{ \AA}$ and an exhaustiveness parameter of \DockExhaustiveness{}, which permitted unrestricted exploration of ligand orientations and rotatable bonds within the site \cite{trott2009}.

Prior to screening the machine learning candidates, the docking protocol was checked by re-docking the co-crystallized \RedockLigand{} ligand into the TgDHFR active site. The top-ranked pose reproduced the crystallographic position with a centroid distance of $\RedockRMSD\text{ \AA}$, below the $\RedockThreshold\text{ \AA}$ criterion adopted for validation. This criterion compares the geometric centers of the docked and crystallographic ligands and is therefore more lenient than a heavy-atom RMSD, which also tests orientation; moreover, the search box was centered on the crystallographic ligand. The check thus confirms that the grid parameters and the docking setup are consistent with the known binding site, but it does not demonstrate blind pose prediction.

Following validation, Vina evaluated all \DockNTotal{} candidates of the screening step (the 10 top-ranked compounds plus the reference entries). For each compound the largest fragment was retained to remove counter-ions, hydrogens were added, a single 3D conformer was generated with ETKDGv3 (fixed seed) and minimized with MMFF, and a flexible-ligand PDBQT file was prepared with Meeko, keeping the protonation state of the input structure. Docking used a fixed random seed and returned the \DockNPoses\ best poses. Poses were ranked by the predicted binding free energy ($\Delta G$) in kcal/mol, where lower energies denote stronger predicted binding \cite{feng2023}; this is an empirical, rigid-receptor estimate and not a measured thermodynamic quantity. The docking ligand efficiency was computed as the best score per heavy atom. To systematically evaluate the consensus between the ligand-based and structure-based approaches, we established quantitative thresholds. Compounds were classified as validated consensus hits only if they exhibited both high ML-predicted potency ($pIC_{50} > \DockMLActiveThresh$) and strong docking affinity ($\Delta G < \DockActiveThresh\text{ kcal/mol}$). Compounds meeting the ML threshold but failing the docking criterion were flagged as potential ML false positives. Conversely, compounds with $pIC_{50} < \DockMLInactiveThresh$ were labeled validated negatives if $\Delta G > \DockInactiveThresh\text{ kcal/mol}$ and potential ML false negatives if $\Delta G < \DockActiveThresh\text{ kcal/mol}$; all remaining compounds were classed as neutral. Finally, to quantify the monotonic agreement between the two orthogonal methods without assuming linearity, a Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted between the continuous $pIC_{50}$ predictions and the Vina docking scores (Section~\ref{sec:consensus_results}).

\subsection{In Silico Pharmacokinetic (ADMET) Profiling}
\label{sec:materials_admet}

Following the docking step, a predictive pharmacokinetic (ADMET) assessment was applied to all \DockNTotal\ docked candidates, not only to the consensus hits, to flag late-stage attrition risks. All structures were reduced to their largest fragment before the calculations, so that counter-ions of salt forms (e.g., glucuronate or hydrochloride) did not distort the descriptor counts or the fingerprints. Candidates first underwent evaluation against three criteria of Lipinski's Rule of Five, eliminating molecules with more than \AdmetLipinskiHBD\ hydrogen bond donors, more than \AdmetLipinskiHBA\ hydrogen bond acceptors, or a calculated LogP (Crippen method) above \AdmetLipinskiLogP; the molecular-weight criterion was not applied because the screening had already capped the molecular weight. To assess critical toxicity and absorption endpoints, models were trained on datasets from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC): \AdmetNHergTrain\ compounds for hERG and \AdmetNCacoTrain\ for Caco-2 permeability (Caco2-Wang). Molecular structures were encoded as 2048-bit Morgan fingerprints (radius 2) and processed through Random Forest models comprising 100 estimators with a fixed random seed.

A \texttt{RandomForestClassifier} trained on the TDC hERG dataset evaluated cardiac toxicity risk. This served as a safety filter, advancing only compounds with a predicted probability of hERG channel blockade below \AdmetHergThresh. Unlike this exclusion criterion, a \texttt{RandomForestRegressor} trained on the Caco2-Wang dataset used intestinal permeability predictions to categorize the likely administration route. Compounds with moderate-to-high predicted permeability ($\log P_{app} > \AdmetCacoThresh$, $P_{app}$ in cm/s) were classified as orally compliant. Conversely, candidates falling below this threshold were not discarded; they were retained and flagged as parenteral candidates, for which poor intestinal absorption is not a limitation.

These models are screening-level estimates rather than experimental toxicity or absorption data, and their predictive performance was not assessed here. Some candidates are also present in the training sets (of the \AdmetNLipinski\ compounds evaluated, \AdmetOverlapHerg\ appear in the hERG set and \AdmetOverlapCaco\ in the Caco-2 set), so their predictions are not independent. Hepatic metabolism, off-target toxicity and blood-brain barrier penetration, which matters for a parasite that infects the brain, were not modeled. The profile therefore prioritizes compounds for further evaluation and does not by itself establish safety.

\subsection{Computational Environment and Reproducibility}

To support algorithmic transparency and computational reproducibility \cite{rojas2026}, all data preparation, model training, and evaluation steps were programmatically implemented in Python (version 3.11.16) and chained in a single entry point that runs the 20 steps of the study, from the construction of the training sets to the final consistency check of the manuscript. The computational environment relied on a standardized core software stack:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Cheminformatics and Featurization:} RDKit (version 2025.9.3) \cite{landrum2025} executed all molecular parsing, structural standardization, and descriptor generation (2D, 3D, and ECFP4); Open Babel (version 3.1.1) \cite{babel2011} converted the receptor to PDBQT.
	\item \textbf{Classical Machine Learning:} The nested cross-validation framework \cite{krstajic2014, calle2025} and baseline algorithms leveraged \texttt{scikit-learn} (version 1.6.0) \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}, \texttt{xgboost} (version 3.1.2) \cite{chengeustrin2016XGBoost}, and \texttt{lightgbm} (version 4.6.0) \cite{kemeng2017LightGBM}. Data manipulation, matrix operations, and statistical tests were managed via \texttt{pandas} (version 2.3.3) \cite{mckinney2010pandas}, \texttt{numpy} (version 1.26.4) \cite{harris2020numpy}, and \texttt{scipy} (version 1.16.3).
	\item \textbf{Deep Learning Infrastructure:} The D-MPNN graph models utilized \texttt{chemprop} (version 2.2.4) \cite{graff2026, heid2024} and \texttt{lightning} (version 2.6.5), with underlying tensor operations supported by PyTorch (\texttt{torch}, version 2.5.1) \cite{paszke2019pytorch}.
	\item \textbf{Docking and ADMET:} AutoDock Vina (Python bindings, version 1.2.7) \cite{trott2009} performed the docking, ligands were prepared with Meeko (version 0.8.0), and the ADMET datasets were loaded through PyTDC (version 0.4.1).
	\item \textbf{Hardware Specifications:} All model training, architecture selection, and evaluation protocols ran on an Ubuntu 26.04 LTS workstation with two Intel(R) Xeon(R) E5-2695 v2 processors (24 physical cores, 48 threads, 2.4 GHz) and 247 GB of RAM, using CPU only; no GPU was involved.
\end{itemize}

All random number generators were seeded (seed 42). The models of the augmentation study were fitted single-threaded, because a multi-threaded random forest sums its trees in a thread-dependent order and changes the last bits of its predictions; parallelism was applied across independent tasks (features in the permutation importance, runs in the ensemble) so that the results do not depend on the number of workers. Together with the numerical tolerance of the selection rule described above, this made the feature selection reproducible: permutation importances computed with 46 and with 8 workers were bitwise identical. The graph-model benchmark is not bit-reproducible, since multi-threaded CPU training of the network varies slightly between executions (mean $R^2$ between 0.725 and 0.729 across three executions). A complete execution from scratch required approximately 30 hours on the workstation, dominated by two nested cross-validation runs of about 13 hours each (main and sensitivity), the y-randomization test (about 2.7 hours), and the graph-model benchmark (about 85 minutes).
