\subsection{Conclusions and Future Directions}

This study compared classical machine learning ensembles with a graph neural network on a small TgDHFR data set ($N = \NCompounds$), under a leakage-free nested cross-validation protocol, and carried the best classical model through a docking and ADMET triage of FDA-approved drugs. Three conclusions follow.

First, on this data set a classical stacking ensemble on 2D Morgan fingerprints performed at least as well as the alternatives we tested. It reached $R^2 = \MorganRTwoMean \pm \MorganRTwoStd$, against $\ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoMean \pm \ChemPropDMPNNFoldRTwoStd$ for a default D-MPNN and $\GnnTunedRTwoMean \pm \GnnTunedRTwoStd$ for one tuned in a nested search; the difference ($\Delta R^2 = \GnnTunedVsMorganDiffRTwo$ for the tuned network) was not significant after multiple-comparison correction, and equivalence held for the MAE but not for $R^2$. Adding 3D descriptors to the fingerprint-based sets changed nothing detectable. Feature selection and Gaussian-noise augmentation, which the original study credited with large gains, did not improve a classical learner under the same hold-out protocol, so those gains were not reproduced. These findings are limited to a single target and a comparison in which the network search was modest; they do not show that graph networks cannot match fixed fingerprints. The classical protocol was also not cheaper to run: the benchmark took longer than the network's, and its advantage lies in accuracy that is not lower, simpler inputs, and a deployed model that needs only a subset of fingerprint bits.

Second, nested cross-validation and a careful curation of the data (removal of duplicates and of two extreme potency values, with a sensitivity analysis of the latter) were necessary to obtain estimates that can be compared across models. Our random-split estimates are also optimistic: with scaffold-grouped splits $R^2$ fell by about 0.2 for both model families (to $\ScaffoldMorganRTwoMean$ for the Morgan ensemble and $\ScaffoldGnnRTwoMean$ for the D-MPNN), and the data could not resolve the difference between them.

Third, combining ML prediction, docking and ADMET produced a short, reproducible list of hypotheses and not validated leads. Of the \ScreenNCandidates{} compounds advanced to docking, \AdmetNConsensus{} unique compounds were consensus hits, and \AdmetNConsensusSurvivors{} of them passed the ADMET filters: pralatrexate, methotrexate and trimetrexate. The agreement between predicted potency and docking energy was weak ($\rho = \DockSpearmanRho$, $p = \DockSpearmanP$), and the most ligand-efficient candidate, triamterene, was flagged by the hERG model. All surviving compounds are antifolates that also inhibit the human enzyme, and selectivity was not assessed.

Future work should therefore (i) dock the consensus hits against human DHFR and test them in enzymatic assays against TgDHFR, (ii) evaluate temporal splits, (iii) compare against larger searches and pretrained graph models, and (iv) apply the pipeline to other targets before claiming that it generalizes.
